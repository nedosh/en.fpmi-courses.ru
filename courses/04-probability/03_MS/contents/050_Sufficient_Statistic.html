Sufficient Statistics and Optimal Estimators

Let us continue discussing properties of estimators. We want to find the best estimator among the unbiased ones. The efficiency criterion does not solve the problem completely: its conditions may fail to hold, while a best estimator may nevertheless exist.

1 Sufficient Statistics

Let \(X\) be an observation (or a sample of observations \(X = (X_1, \ldots , X_n)\)) with unknown distribution \(\mathbb {P} \in \mathcal{P}=\left\{ \mathbb {P}_{\theta }\right\}_{\theta \in \Theta }\).

Definition 1 A statistic \(S(X)\) is sufficient for the family of distributions \(\mathcal{P}\) if the conditional distribution \(\mathbb {P}_{\theta }\left(X \in B \mid S(X)=x\right)\) does not depend on \(\theta\).

A sufficient statistic contains all the information about the unknown parameter that the sample can give us. The most obvious example of a sufficient statistic is the sample \(X\) itself. However, as a rule, there exist sufficient statistics of dimension much smaller than the sample. In this case, when conducting some long-term study, after collecting the data one can compute the statistic found and store it, discarding the data itself, thereby greatly reducing the amount of memory needed to store the results of the study.

Next we will formulate a criterion for a statistic to be sufficient, but first, in order to illustrate this concept, let us solve a problem on checking sufficiency.

ExampleExample 1

Let \(X_{1}, \ldots , X_{n}\) be a sample from the distribution \(\operatorname {Bern}(\theta )\). Prove that \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}\) is a sufficient statistic.

SolutionSolution

Let us check the definition of a sufficient statistic: \[ \begin{align} \mathbb {P}\left(X_{1}=x_{1}, \ldots , X_{n}=x_{n} \mid S(X)=s\right)= \\ =\frac{\mathbb {P}\left(X_{1}=x_{1}, \ldots , X_{n}=x_{n}, \sum X_{i}=s\right)}{\mathbb {P}\left(\sum X_{i}=s\right)}= \\ =\; \mathbb {1}_{\sum _{i=1}^{n} x_{i}=s} \frac{\theta ^{\sum x_{i}}(1-\theta )^{n-\sum x_{i}}}{C_{n}^{s} \theta ^{s}(1-\theta )^{n-s}} . \end{align} \] On the support of the indicator \(\sum x_i = s\), so \(\theta^{\sum x_i}(1-\theta )^{n-\sum x_i}=\theta^{s}(1-\theta )^{n-s}\) and these factors cancel with the denominator: \[ \mathbb {P}\left(X_{1}=x_{1}, \ldots , X_{n}=x_{n} \mid S(X)=s\right)=\frac{1}{C_{n}^{s}} \; \mathbb {1}_{\sum x_{i}=s} . \] We see that the conditional distribution does not depend on \(\theta\), hence the statistic \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}\) is sufficient for the family of distributions \(\operatorname {Bern}(\theta )\).

Theorem 1 (The Neyman-Fisher Factorization Criterion) Let \(\mathcal{P}=\left\{ \mathbb {P}_{\theta }, \theta \in \Theta \right\}\) be a dominated family of distributions \(\mathbb {P}_{\theta }\) with “generalized” density \(p_{\theta }\) (in the case of a discrete distribution, \(\left.p_{\theta }(x)=\mathbb {P}_{\theta }(X=x)\right)\), and let \(X\) be an observation with unknown distribution \(\mathbb {P}_{\theta } \in \mathcal{P}\). Then \(S(X)\) is a sufficient statistic if and only if the “generalized” density (likelihood function) admits a representation \[ p_{\theta }(X)=\psi (S(X), \theta ) h(X), \] where the function \(h\) does not depend on the parameter \(\theta\).

ExampleExample 2

Let \(X_{1}, \ldots , X_{n}\) be a sample from \(\mathscr {N}\left(a, \sigma^2\right)\), \(\theta =\left(a, \sigma^{2}\right)\). Find a sufficient statistic.

SolutionSolution

Let us find the likelihood of a sample from \(\mathcal{N}\left(a, \sigma^{2}\right)\):

\[ \begin{align} f_{\theta }\left(X_{1}, \ldots , X_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2 \pi \sigma ^{2}\right)^{n / 2}} \exp \left\{ -\frac{1}{2 \sigma ^{2}} \sum \left(X_{i}-a\right)^{2}\right\} . \end{align} \]

Expand the brackets in the exponent: \(\sum \left(X_i-a\right)^2 = \sum X_i^2 - 2a\sum X_i + na^2\), whence

\[ \begin{align} f_{\theta }\left(X_{1}, \ldots , X_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2 \pi \sigma ^{2}\right)^{n / 2}} \exp \left\{ -\frac{1}{2 \sigma ^{2}} \sum X_{i}^{2}+\frac{a}{\sigma ^{2}} \sum X_{i}-\frac{n a^{2}}{2 \sigma ^{2}}\right\} . \end{align} \]

Setting \(h(x)=1\),

\[ \psi \left(s_{1}, s_{2}, \theta \right)=\frac{1}{\left(2 \pi \sigma ^{2}\right)^{n / 2}} \exp \left\{ -\frac{1}{2 \sigma ^{2}} s_{1}+\frac{a}{\sigma ^{2}} s_{2}-\frac{n a^{2}}{2 \sigma ^{2}}\right\} \]

and applying the factorization criterion, we obtain that the statistic \(S(X)=\left(\sum X_{i}^{2}, \sum X_{i}\right)\) is sufficient.

Note. Note that any bijection of a sufficient statistic is also sufficient. In this case, for example, besides \(\left(\sum X_{i}^{2}, \sum X_{i}\right)\), sufficient statistics also include \(\left(\overline{X}, \overline{X^2}\right)\) and \(\left(\overline{X}, s^2\right)\).

ProblemProblem 1

Find sufficient statistics for the following parametric distributions:

  1. \(N(a, \sigma^2)\);

  2. \(\Gamma (\alpha , \lambda )\);

  3. \(U(a,b)\);

  4. \(\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda )\);

  5. \(\mathrm{Ber}(p)\);

  6. \(\mathrm{Geom}(p)\).

Note. Each part is worth \(0.3\) points.

Theorem 2 (Rao-Blackwell-Kolmogorov) Let \(S(X)\) be a sufficient statistic, \(\widehat{\tau }\) an unbiased estimator of \(\tau (\theta )\), square-integrable for every \(\theta \in \Theta\). Then \(\tau^{*}=\mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\widehat{\tau } \mid S(X)\right]\) is also an unbiased estimator of \(\tau (\theta )\), which is uniformly better than \(\widehat{\tau }\): \[ \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\left(\tau ^{*} - \tau (\theta )\right)^2\right] = \operatorname {Var}_{\theta }\left[\tau ^*\right] \leq \operatorname {Var}_{\theta }\left[\widehat{\tau }\right] = \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\left(\widehat{\tau } - \tau (\theta )\right)^2\right], \quad \forall \theta \in \Theta \]

The theorem is a direct consequence of the properties of conditional expectation. The transition from an unbiased estimator \(\widehat{\tau }\) to \(\tau^{*}=\mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\widehat{\tau } \mid S(X)\right]\) is called Rao-Blackwellization or the Rao-Blackwell improvement of the estimator \(\widehat{\tau }\).

Note that this theorem only allows us to improve the risk function of an unbiased estimator \(\hat{\theta }\), and it allows us to find the best estimator only if an additional condition, called completeness, is imposed on the statistic \(S(X)\). If completeness is absent, then in some cases the Rao-Blackwell-Kolmogorov improvement may fail to be optimal, as the following problem shows.

ProblemProblem 2

Let \(X_1, \ldots , X_n\) be a sample from the distribution \(U[(1-k)\theta , (1+k)\theta ]\), with unknown parameter \(\mathbb {E}\left[X_1\right] = \theta > 0\) and known parameter \(k \in (0,1)\).

  1. Prove that \(\widehat{\theta }_1 := X_1, \; \widehat{\theta }_2 := \overline{X}\) are unbiased estimators of \(\theta\). Compute their variances;

  2. Prove that \(T(X) := (X_{(1)}, X_{(n)})^T\) is a sufficient statistic;

  3. Prove that \(\theta^*_1 := \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\widehat{\theta }_1 \mid (X_{(1)}, X_{(n)})\right] = \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\widehat{\theta }_2 \mid (X_{(1)}, X_{(n)})\right] = \frac{X_{(1)} + X_{(n)}}{2}\);

  4. Compute the risk function (variance) of the estimator \(\theta^*_1\). Show that this estimator, being the Rao-Blackwell-Kolmogorov improvement of \(\widehat{\theta }_1\) and \(\widehat{\theta }_2\), is uniformly better than \(\widehat{\theta }_1, \widehat{\theta }_2\).

  5. Consider the estimator

    \[ \theta _2^*(X) := \frac{1}{2\left(k^2 \frac{n-1}{n+1} + 1\right)} \left[(1-k)X_{(1)} + (1+k)X_{(n)}\right] \]

    Prove that \(\theta_2^*\) is also an unbiased estimator of \(\theta\). Show that it is uniformly better than \(\theta_1^*\).

    Summarizing the previous parts, one needs to show that

    \[ \theta _2^* \prec \theta _1^* \prec \widehat{\theta }_2 \prec \widehat{\theta }_1, \]

    where \(T_1 \prec T_2\) means that the estimator \(T_1\) is better than \(T_2\) in the uniform approach with quadratic loss function.

  6. Prove that the statistic \(T = (X_{(1)}, X_{(n)})^T\) is not complete (see the definition in the following sections), and hence \(\theta^*_1\) is not an optimal estimator.

*Note.** The whole problem is worth 3 points. If you solve it, the solution should be sent via Telegram.

2 Complete Statistics

Definition 2 A statistic \(S(X)\) is called complete for \(\left\{ \mathbb {P}_{\theta }, \theta \in \Theta \right\}\) if for all functions \(f(x)\) with the property \[ \forall \theta \in \Theta \quad \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[f(S(X))\right]=0 \] we have \(f(S(X)) = 0\) \(\mathbb {P}_\theta\)-a.s. \(\forall \theta \in \Theta\). That is, \(S\) is a complete statistic if there does not exist a function of it \(f(S)\) that would be a nontrivial (not identically zero) unbiased estimator of zero.

Completeness is needed for the Lehmann-Scheffé theorem, which states that if, under the conditions of the Rao-Blackwell-Kolmogorov theorem, one takes not merely a sufficient statistic \(S\) but a complete and sufficient one, then the estimator obtained after Rao-Blackwellization will not just be better than the original one in the mean-squared sense, but will turn out to be the very best one, i.e. optimal. More on this in the next section. For now let us understand completeness a bit better.

ExampleExample 3

Prove that the statistic \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}\) is not only sufficient but also complete for the family of distributions \(\operatorname {Bern}(\theta ), \theta \in \Theta = (0,1)\).

Prove that the two-dimensional statistic \((\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}, X_1)\) is sufficient but is not complete.

SolutionSolution

Sufficiency was proved above.

Completeness: we need to prove that if for all \(\theta \in (0,1)\) and some function \(f(x)\) we have \(\mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[f\left(\sum X_{i}\right)\right]=0\), then \(f(x)=0\) for all \(x \in \{ 0,1, \ldots , n\}\). We have

\[ \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[f\left(\sum X_{i}\right)\right]=\sum _{k=0}^{n} f(k) C_{n}^{k} \theta ^{k}(1-\theta )^{n-k} . \]

We obtain on the right-hand side a polynomial of degree at most \(n\) in \(\theta\). If not all its coefficients are 0, then it has at most \(n\) roots on \((0,1)\). But this polynomial equals 0 for all \(\theta \in (0,1)\), i.e. all \(\theta\) in the interval \((0,1)\) are its roots. Hence, all coefficients of this polynomial equal 0, and therefore \(f(k)=0\) for all \(k \in \{ 0, \ldots , n\}\).

For the statistic \((\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}, X_1)\): it is sufficient, since its first component is sufficient, and sufficiency clearly cannot be lost by extending a statistic. Completeness: let us construct from it the estimator

\[ T(X) = \sum _{i=1}^{n} X_{i} - nX_1 \]

This estimator is an unbiased estimator of zero: for all \(\theta \in (0,1)\) we have

\[ \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[T\right] = \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\sum _{i=1}^{n} X_{i} - nX_1\right] = n\theta - n\theta = 0. \]

At the same time it is nontrivial, i.e. is not almost surely equal to zero for any \(\theta\). Hence, \((\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}, X_1)\) is not complete.

As can be seen from the example, increasing the dimension of a statistic, adding new components to it, can lead to a loss of completeness (sufficiency, however, is preserved if it was present to begin with). At the same time, completeness is more a property of the model than a property of the statistic. This can be clearly seen by solving the problem below.

ProblemProblem 3

Give an example of a parametric family of distributions \(\mathcal{P}\) and a nontrivial, incomplete, sufficient statistic \(S(X_1, \ldots , X_n)\) of dimension 1, where \(X_1, \ldots , X_n\) is a sample from an unknown distribution \(\mathbb {P} \in \mathcal{P}\).

HintHint

Take \(\mathcal{P}\) to be the collection of distributions \(\operatorname {Bern}(\theta )\), as in the example above. But shrink the parameter set: let \(\Theta = \left\{ \frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3}\right\}\).

Thus, completeness can be lost either by extending a statistic or by shrinking the parameter set.

Of course, the statistic \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}\) is far from always complete. So how does one find a complete statistic? Proving completeness directly from the definition is, as a rule, quite difficult. However, as we shall see below, for a sufficiently wide class of distributions a complete statistic can be found simply from the likelihood function.

Recall the notion of an exponential family, introduced in detail in §exponential families: a collection of distributions \(\mathcal{P} = \left\{ \mathbb {P}_{\theta }, \theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb {R}^{k}\right\}\) is called an exponential family if its generalized density has the form

\[ p_{\theta }(x)=h(x) \exp \left(\sum _{i=1}^{k} a_{i}(\theta ) u_{i}(x)+v(\theta )\right) , \tag{1}\]

where the number of terms \(a_i(\theta )u_i(x)\) does not exceed the dimension of \(\Theta\), and the support of the distribution does not depend on the parameter. It was proved there that the normal, exponential, binomial, Poisson, and many other distributions belong to the exponential family (see the example with \(\mathscr {N}\left(\mu , \sigma^2\right)\)).

Theorem 3 (on exponential families) Let \(\theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb {R}^{k}\) and let condition Equation 1 hold for the family of distributions \(\left\{ \mathbb {P}_{\theta }, \theta \in \Theta \right\}\). Suppose, in addition, that the set of all values of the vector \(\left(a_{1}(\theta ), \ldots , a_{k}(\theta )\right)\) for \(\theta \in \Theta\) contains a \(k\)-dimensional ball of the space \(\mathbb {R}^{k}\). Then \(S(X)=\left(\overline{u_{1}(X)}, \ldots , \overline{u_{k}(X)}\right)\) is a complete sufficient statistic.

For the normal distribution, for example, the statistic \((\overline{X}, \overline{X^2})\) will be complete and sufficient. Note also that any bijective function preserves completeness and sufficiency. That is, for example, for the normal distribution the statistic \((\overline{X}, s^2) = (\overline{X}, \overline{X^2} - \overline{X}^2)\) will also be complete and sufficient.

It is worth noting that the condition of the theorem holds if the set \(\Theta\) is “solid,” i.e. contains its interior points, and the functions \(a_{1}(\theta ), \ldots , a_{k}(\theta )\) are linearly independent and smooth in a neighborhood of some interior point \(\theta_{0} \in \Theta\) (more on this — in terms of full-rank and curved exponential families — see §exponential families).

3 Optimal Estimators

In this section we finally give an algorithm for finding the estimators that are best in the sense mentioned, which are customarily called optimal.

Definition 3 A statistic \(\tau^{*}\) is called an optimal estimator of \(\tau (\theta )\) if \(\tau^{*}\) is the best estimator of \(\tau (\theta )\) in the mean-squared approach within the class of unbiased estimators (note that \(\tau (\theta )\) may, generally speaking, have dimension greater than 1).

Theorem 4 (Lehmann-Scheffé) Let \(S(X)\) be a complete sufficient statistic, \(\widehat{\tau }\) an unbiased estimator of \(\tau (\theta )\); then \(\tau^{*}=\mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\widehat{\tau } \mid S(X)\right]\) is an optimal estimator of \(\tau (\theta )\).

Recall that the conditional expectation with respect to a random variable (or vector) \(S\) can always be represented as a Borel function of that variable (respectively, vector):

\[ \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\widehat{\tau } \mid S(X)\right] = \varphi (S(X)) \quad \mathbb {P}_\theta \text{-a.s.} \]

From this follows an alternative formulation of the Lehmann-Scheffé theorem.

Lehmann-Scheffé Any Borel function of a complete, sufficient statistic is an optimal estimator of its own expectation.

Algorithm for finding an optimal estimator:

  1. Either look for a sufficient statistic \(S(X)\) using the factorization criterion and prove that it is complete, or find a complete sufficient statistic directly, if the given family of distributions is exponential.

  2. Solve the “unbiasedness” equation \(\mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\varphi (S(X))\right]=\tau (\theta )\) for \(\varphi\) (for every \(\theta \in \Theta\)). Its solution \(\varphi (S(X))\) is an optimal estimator of \(\tau (\theta )\).

Unbiased estimator

A statistic \(\widehat{\tau}\) is called an unbiased estimator of \(\tau(\theta)\) if \(\mathbb{E}_\theta[\widehat{\tau}] = \tau(\theta)\) for every \(\theta \in \Theta\). This is the starting point of the pipeline — literally any unbiased estimator will do, even an inefficient one: for example, for \(\mathrm{Bern}(\theta)\) one can simply take \(\widehat{\tau} = X_1\) (unbiased, but with large variance).

The Rao-Blackwell-Kolmogorov theorem

Let \(S(X)\) be a sufficient statistic, \(\widehat{\tau}\) an unbiased estimator of \(\tau(\theta)\), square-integrable for every \(\theta \in \Theta\). Then \(\tau^{*}=\mathbb{E}_\theta[\widehat{\tau} \mid S(X)]\) is also an unbiased estimator of \(\tau(\theta)\), which is uniformly better than \(\widehat{\tau}\): \[ \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[(\tau^{*} - \tau(\theta))^2\right] = \operatorname{Var}_\theta(\tau^*) \le \operatorname{Var}_\theta(\widehat{\tau}) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[(\widehat{\tau} - \tau(\theta))^2\right], \quad \forall \theta \in \Theta. \] Averaging over a sufficient statistic ("Rao-Blackwellization") never increases the risk.

Completeness of a statistic

A statistic \(S(X)\) is called complete if for any Borel function \(f\), \(\mathbb{E}_\theta[f(S(X))] = 0\) for all \(\theta \in \Theta\) implies \(f(S(X)) = 0\) \(\;\mathbb{P}_\theta\)-a.s. for all \(\theta\) — that is, a complete statistic has no nontrivial unbiased estimators of zero. Completeness is primarily a property of the model (the parametric family), not just of the statistic itself: one and the same statistic can be complete for one model and cease to be complete for a restricted parameter set of the same model.

If \(S\) is not complete: no guarantees

Example: \(X_1,\dots,X_n \sim U[(1-k)\theta,(1+k)\theta]\), \(k \in (0,1)\) known, \(\theta > 0\) unknown. The statistic \(S=(X_{(1)}, X_{(n)})\) is sufficient (by the factorization criterion), but not complete. Rao-Blackwellizing either of the unbiased estimators \(\widehat\theta_1=X_1\) or \(\widehat\theta_2=\overline{X}\) gives the same improved estimator \(\theta^{*}=\dfrac{X_{(1)}+X_{(n)}}{2}\), uniformly better than the originals — but since \(S\) is not complete, the Lehmann-Scheffé theorem does not apply: one cannot prove optimality of \(\theta^{*}\) within the class of all unbiased estimators, and in principle there may exist an estimator uniformly better than \(\theta^{*}\).

The Lehmann-Scheffé theorem

Let \(S(X)\) be a complete sufficient statistic, \(\widehat{\tau}\) an unbiased estimator of \(\tau(\theta)\). Then \(\tau^{*}=\mathbb{E}_\theta[\widehat{\tau} \mid S(X)]\) is an optimal estimator of \(\tau(\theta)\): the best in the mean-squared sense among all unbiased estimators, and the result is the same for any starting \(\widehat\tau\). Equivalent formulation: any Borel function of a complete sufficient statistic is an optimal estimator of its own expectation. So in practice there is no need to look for \(\widehat\tau\) at all — one can directly solve the unbiasedness equation \(\mathbb{E}_\theta[\varphi(S)]=\tau(\theta)\) for \(\varphi\).

ExampleExample 4

Let \(X_{1}, \ldots X_{n}\) be a sample from the uniform distribution on \([0, \theta ]\). Find an optimal estimator of the parameter \(\theta\).

SolutionSolution

The likelihood function equals \(\frac{1}{\theta^{n}} \; \mathbb {1}_{X_{(n)} \leq \theta }\), so, by the factorization criterion, \(X_{(n)}\) is a sufficient statistic. Let us check that this statistic is complete:

\[ \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[f\left(X_{(n)}\right)\right]=\int _{0}^{\theta } f(x) n \frac{x^{n-1}}{\theta ^{n}} d x \]

By a property of the Riemann integral, the last expression equals 0 for every \(\theta >0\) if and only if \(f(x) x^{n-1}=0\) for almost every \(x>0\) (the Lebesgue measure of the set of such \(x\) for which the last expression is nonzero equals 0). Hence \(f(x)=0\) (\(\mathbb {P}_{\theta }\)-a.s.) for all \(\theta \in \Theta\).

So, we know that \(X_{(n)}\) is a complete sufficient statistic. It remains to solve the “unbiasedness” equation \(\mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[\varphi \left(X_{(n)}\right)\right]=\theta\). As is known,

\[ \mathbb {E}_{\theta }\left[X_{(n)}\right]=\frac{n}{n+1} \theta . \]

Hence, as a solution to the equation we may propose the function \(\varphi (x)=\frac{n+1}{n} x\). Consequently, the estimator \(\frac{n+1}{n} X_{(n)}\) is optimal.

Let \(X_1, \dots , X_n\) be a sample from the Poisson distribution with parameter \(\theta > 0\). Find \[ \mathbb {E}\left[X_1^2 \; \bigg| \; \sum _{i=1}^n X_i\right]. \]
