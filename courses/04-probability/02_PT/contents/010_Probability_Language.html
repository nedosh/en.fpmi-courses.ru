The Language of Probability Theory

In the ETMT course we built measure theory: we learned to extend measures from semirings to \(\sigma\)-algebras, to construct Lebesgue and Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure, and to integrate measurable functions. Let us now move on to obtaining practical results, translating all of elementary probability theory into the language of measure theory.

1 Why probability needs measure theory

1.1 Three reasons

One might object: probabilities were successfully computed even before Kolmogorov — Pascal, Bernoulli, and Laplace managed without \(\sigma\)-algebras. Why do we need this complicated language? There are three fundamental reasons.

Reason 1: continuous variables. Let \(\xi\) be the coordinate of a random point on the interval \([0,1]\). For any point \(x\) it is reasonable to set \(\mathbb {P}\left(\xi = x\right) = 0\). But then “the probability of landing in a set \(B\)” cannot be assembled from the probabilities of individual points: a sum of zeros gives zero, while \(\mathbb {P}\left(\xi \in [0,1]\right) = 1\). Probability in the continuous world is fundamentally not a sum over points, but a measure of a set. Without measure theory we cannot even say which sets \(B\) are allowed to be assigned a probability at all (recall the non-measurable Vitali set from ETMT: there exist sets that cannot be assigned a probability without destroying the axioms).

Reason 2: infinite sequences of experiments. All the main theorems of this course — the laws of large numbers, the Borel-Cantelli lemma, the central limit theorem — are statements about infinite sequences of random variables. For instance, the strong law of large numbers asserts:

\[ \mathbb {P}\left(\frac{\xi _1 + \ldots + \xi _n}{n} \xrightarrow [n \to \infty ]{} \mathbb {E}\left[\xi _1\right]\right) = 1. \]

The event under the probability is an event about the entire infinite trajectory \((\xi_1, \xi_2, \ldots )\); it cannot be expressed through finitely many experiments. For such events to have a probability, we need a \(\sigma\)-algebra (countable operations on events!) and countable additivity of the measure.

Reason 3: a unified language. In elementary probability theory, the discrete and continuous cases are two separate theories: one uses sums, the other integrals; one uses distribution tables, the other densities; every statement is proved twice. Measure theory glues them into a single theory: everything is a Lebesgue integral, and sums and Riemann integrals are its special cases. Moreover, distributions will appear later that are neither discrete nor continuous, and this is not exotica: insurance, for instance, supplies them routinely (see the example with an insurance payout at the end of this topic) — and elementary theory has no tools for them whatsoever.

1.2 Dictionary

Kolmogorov’s axiomatics (1933) is, in essence, a translation dictionary: probabilistic notions are defined as notions of measure theory. We will use this dictionary throughout the semester:

Dictionary: measure theory \(\leftrightarrow\) probability theory
Measurable space\((\Omega , \mathcal{F})\) Outcomes and events
Measure\(\mu\) with\(\mu (\Omega ) = 1\) Probability\(\mathbb {P}\)
Measurable set\(A \in \mathcal{F}\) Event
Measurable function\(\xi \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}\) Random variable
Pushforward measure\(\mathbb {P} \circ \xi ^{-1}\) Distribution\(\mathbb {P}_\xi\)
Lebesgue integral\(\int _\Omega \xi \, d\mathbb {P}\) Expectation\(\mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right]\)
Almost everywhere Almost surely (a.s.)
Convergence in measure Convergence in probability
\(L^p(\Omega , \mathcal{F}, \mathbb {P})\) Variables with finite\(p\)-th moment
Radon-Nikodym derivative Density (likelihood)
Fubini’s theorem Independence, convolution

The right-hand column is not merely a renaming. As the saying goes, “probability theory is measure theory with a soul”: probabilistic notions have substantive interpretations (frequencies, forecasts, risks), and there are concepts of their own that measure theory does not have — above all independence and conditional constructions. It is precisely these that turn measure theory into a working tool: insurance companies compute reserves as expectations, financial mathematics builds prices of derivative instruments via a change of measure (that very Radon-Nikodym derivative), statistics and machine learning maximize likelihood — a density in the Radon-Nikodym sense. All of this is the dictionary above, applied in earnest.

2 Probability space

Definition 1 A probability space is a triple \((\Omega , \mathcal{F}, \mathbb {P})\), where

  • \(\Omega\) is a nonempty set (the sample space);

  • \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra of subsets of \(\Omega\) (its elements are events);

  • \(\mathbb {P}\colon \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]\) is a measure with \(\mathbb {P}(\Omega ) = 1\) (probability).

All properties of probability are properties of a finite measure, proved in ETMT. Let us collect them in one place.

Proposition 1 (Equivalent conditions for a probability) Let \(\mathbb {P}\) be a nonnegative, non-degenerate, additive function on a ring (in particular, on a sigma-algebra). Then the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. …

  2. (countable subadditivity) \(\mathbb {P}\left(\bigcup_{n} A_n\right) \leq \sum_{n} \mathbb {P}\left(A_n\right)\);

  3. (continuity from below) if \(A_n \uparrow A\) (i.e. \(A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \ldots\) and \(A = \bigcup_n A_n\)), then \(\mathbb {P}\left(A_n\right) \uparrow \mathbb {P}\left(A\right)\);

  4. (continuity at zero)

  5. (continuity from above) if \(A_n \downarrow A\), then \(\mathbb {P}\left(A_n\right) \downarrow \mathbb {P}\left(A\right)\);

Proposition 2 (Properties of probability) Let \(A, B, A_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}\). Then:

  1. \(\mathbb {P}\left(A^\complement \right) = 1 - \mathbb {P}\left(A\right)\); if \(A \subseteq B\), then \(\mathbb {P}\left(A\right) \leq \mathbb {P}\left(B\right)\);

  2. (inclusion-exclusion formula)

    \[ \mathbb {P}\left(\bigcup _{i=1}^n A_i\right) = \sum _i \mathbb {P}\left(A_i\right) - \sum _{i<j} \mathbb {P}\left(A_i \cap A_j\right) + \ldots + (-1)^{n-1}\mathbb {P}\left(A_1 \cap \ldots \cap A_n\right). \]

…

The main supplier of nontrivial probability spaces is Carathéodory’s theorem. Let us work through the most important example: an infinite sequence of coin tosses.

ExampleExample 1

(The infinite Bernoulli scheme.) Construct a probability space on which an infinite sequence of independent coin tosses with success probability \(p\) is defined.

SolutionSolution

Take \(\Omega = \left\{ 0,1\right\}^\infty = \left\{ \omega = (\omega_1, \omega_2, \ldots ) \colon \omega_i \in \left\{ 0,1\right\} \right\}\) — the space of all infinite binary sequences. An elementary outcome is the entire infinite trajectory of the experiment.

Next we need a \(\sigma\)-algebra. The natural “observable” events are cylinders: events depending on only finitely many coordinates,

\[ C_{a_1 \ldots a_n} = \left\{ \omega \colon \omega _1 = a_1, \ldots , \omega _n = a_n\right\} , \qquad a_i \in \left\{ 0,1\right\} . \]

Finite unions of cylinders form an algebra \(\mathcal{A}\); set \(\mathcal{F} = \sigma (\mathcal{A})\).

On cylinders, the probability is dictated by independence of the tosses:

\[ \mathbb {P}\left(C_{a_1 \ldots a_n}\right) = p^{\sum _i a_i}(1-p)^{n - \sum _i a_i}. \]

This defines a finitely additive function on the algebra \(\mathcal{A}\); its countable additivity on \(\mathcal{A}\) is a separate lemma (using compactness), after which Carathéodory’s theorem extends \(\mathbb {P}\) to \(\mathcal{F} = \sigma (\mathcal{A})\), and moreover uniquely.

Notice that meaningful events — for instance,

\[ A = \left\{ \text{the fraction of successes in the first } n \text{ tosses converges to } p \text{ as } n \to \infty \right\} \]

— are not cylinders; they depend on the entire infinite trajectory. But they are obtained from cylinders by countable operations, so they lie in \(\mathcal{F}\), and they do have a probability. The assertion \(\mathbb {P}\left(A\right) = 1\) is the strong law of large numbers (topic 6). The whole construction exists precisely so that such a question makes sense at all.

3 Independence

Independence is the first notion absent from measure theory: it is the genuine contribution of probability theory itself. Let us recall the elementary definition and immediately lift it to the level of generality we need.

Definition 2 Events \(A_1, \ldots , A_n\) are jointly independent if for every collection of indices \(i_1 < \ldots < i_k\) \[ \mathbb {P}\left(A_{i_1} \cap \ldots \cap A_{i_k}\right) = \mathbb {P}\left(A_{i_1}\right) \cdots \mathbb {P}\left(A_{i_k}\right). \] Systems of events \(\mathcal{M}_1, \ldots , \mathcal{M}_n \subseteq \mathcal{F}\) are independent if any \(A_1 \in \mathcal{M}_1, \ldots , A_n \in \mathcal{M}_n\) are independent. Random variables \(\xi_1, \ldots , \xi_n\) are independent if the \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\sigma (\xi_1), \ldots , \sigma (\xi_n)\) they generate are independent, where \(\sigma (\xi ) = \left\{ \left\{ \xi \in B\right\} \colon B \in \mathscr {B}\left(\mathbb {R}\right)\right\}\).

The definition of independence of variables requires checking factorization for all Borel sets — an unmanageably large number. The working tool that reduces the check to a manageable collection is \(\pi\)-systems.

Definition 3 A system of sets \(\mathcal{M}\) is called a \(\pi\)-system if it is closed under finite intersections: \(A, B \in \mathcal{M} \Rightarrow A \cap B \in \mathcal{M}\).

Theorem 1 (Independence criterion via \(\pi\)-systems) Let \(\mathcal{M}_1, \ldots , \mathcal{M}_n\) be independent \(\pi\)-systems. Then the \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\sigma (\mathcal{M}_1), \ldots , \sigma (\mathcal{M}_n)\) are also independent.

The proof is Dynkin’s \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem (also known as the monotone class theorem from ETMT; see also [@shiryaev2016probability], Ch. 2, §2): with events from the other systems fixed, the collection of sets for which factorization holds forms a \(\lambda\)-system containing the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{M}_i\), and hence also \(\sigma (\mathcal{M}_i)\).

The main consequence: it suffices to check independence of variables on rays \(\left\{ \xi \leq x\right\}\) — they form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\sigma (\xi )\).

Corollary 1 Random variables \(\xi_1, \ldots , \xi_n\) are independent if and only if for all \(x_1, \ldots , x_n \in \mathbb {R}\) \[ \mathbb {P}\left(\xi _1 \leq x_1, \ldots , \xi _n \leq x_n\right) = \prod _{i=1}^n \mathbb {P}\left(\xi _i \leq x_i\right). \]

Corollary 2 If \(\xi_1, \ldots , \xi_n\) are independent and \(f_1, \ldots , f_n\colon \mathbb {R} \to \mathbb {R}\) are Borel functions, then \(f_1(\xi_1), \ldots , f_n(\xi_n)\) are independent. More generally: Borel functions of disjoint groups of independent variables are independent (for instance, for independent \(\xi_1, \ldots , \xi_4\), the variables \(\xi_1 + \xi_2\) and \(\xi_3 \xi_4\) are independent).

Practical meaning: independence is a modeling assumption that we constantly impose on reality (independent clients of an insurance company, independent measurements in an experiment, independent requests to a server). Theorem Theorem 1 makes this assumption usable: it suffices to control factorization on simple events, and it extends automatically to everything else. The Monte Carlo method also rests on this theorem: a generator produces independent \(\operatorname {U}[0,1]\) variables, and everything we build from them via functions (corollary Corollary 2) remains independent.

4 Random variables and their distributions

Definition 4 A random variable on \((\Omega , \mathcal{F}, \mathbb {P})\) is a measurable function \(\xi \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}\), i.e. one such that \(\left\{ \xi \in B\right\} := \xi^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{F}\) for every \(B \in \mathscr {B}\left(\mathbb {R}\right)\).

Measurability is not a technical formality: it is the requirement that the question “what is the probability that \(\xi\) lands in \(B\)?” have an answer. The entire theory of measurable functions from ETMT now works for free: sums, products, and pointwise limits of random variables are again random variables.

Definition 5 The distribution of a random variable \(\xi\) is the pushforward of the measure \(\mathbb {P}\) under the map \(\xi\): \[ \mathbb {P}_\xi (B) = \mathbb {P}\left(\xi \in B\right), \qquad B \in \mathscr {B}\left(\mathbb {R}\right). \] This is a probability measure on \((\mathbb {R}, \mathscr {B}\left(\mathbb {R}\right))\). The distribution function (CDF) is \(F_\xi (x) = \mathbb {P}\left(\xi \leq x\right)\).

Here an important shift of viewpoint occurs, on which all practical work with probabilities is built. The space \((\Omega , \mathcal{F}, \mathbb {P})\) is “backstage”: it models the source of randomness, and its inner workings are usually unknown, and indeed unimportant. The distribution \(\mathbb {P}_\xi\) is what is “on stage”: a measure on the real line, with which we actually compute. The whole point of the concept of distribution is to transfer computations from the abstract \(\Omega\) to \(\mathbb {R}\), where we have analysis: CDFs, densities, integrals. The statement that makes this viewpoint legitimate was proved in ETMT:

Theorem 2 (Correspondence theorem) The formula \(F(x) = \mathbb {P}_\xi \left((-\infty , x]\right)\) establishes a bijection between probability measures on \((\mathbb {R}, \mathscr {B}\left(\mathbb {R}\right))\) and functions \(F\colon \mathbb {R} \to [0,1]\) that are non-decreasing, right-continuous, and have limits \(F(-\infty ) = 0\), \(F(+\infty ) = 1\); the measure is recovered from \(F\) as a Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure (Shiryaev [@shiryaev2016probability], Ch. 2, §3).

Thus the CDF is a complete passport of the distribution: two distributions coincide if and only if their CDFs coincide. (Why? Again \(\pi\)-systems: the rays \((-\infty , x]\) form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathscr {B}\left(\mathbb {R}\right)\), and two measures that agree on it agree on the whole \(\sigma\)-algebra.)

4.1 Types of distributions

By the Lebesgue decomposition theorem, every CDF can be represented as a mixture \[ F = \alpha _1 F_{\text{d}} + \alpha _2 F_{\text{ac}} + \alpha _3 F_{\text{sing}}, \qquad \alpha _i \geq 0, \quad \alpha _1 + \alpha _2 + \alpha _3 = 1, \] where \(F_{\text{d}}\) is discrete (step-like), \(F_{\text{ac}}\) is absolutely continuous (has a density), and \(F_{\text{sing}}\) is singular (continuous, but increasing on a set of Lebesgue measure zero; the standard example is the Cantor staircase from ETMT). The discrete and absolutely continuous distributions of the elementary course are only the extreme cases.

5 Expectation

Definition 6 The expectation of a random variable \(\xi\) is its Lebesgue integral with respect to the probability measure: \[ \mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right] = \int _\Omega \xi (\omega ) \, \mathbb {P}(d\omega ), \] defined when \(\mathbb {E}\left[\left|\xi \right|\right] < \infty\) (and for nonnegative \(\xi\) — always, with value in \([0, +\infty ]\)).

The construction of the integral is from ETMT, in three steps: indicators and simple functions \(\to\) nonnegative functions (supremum over simple functions from below) \(\to\) the general case via \(\xi = \xi^+ - \xi^-\). From it, all the properties of the Lebesgue integral are inherited: linearity, monotonicity, and — most important for this course — limit theorems:

Proposition 3 (Limit theorems for the expectation) Let \(\xi_n \to \xi\) a.s. Then:

  1. (monotone convergence) if \(0 \leq \xi_n \uparrow \xi\) a.s., then \(\mathbb {E}\left[\xi_n\right] \uparrow \mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right]\);

  2. (Fatou’s lemma) if \(\xi_n \geq 0\), then \(\mathbb {E}\left[\liminf_n \xi_n\right] \leq \liminf_n \mathbb {E}\left[\xi_n\right]\);

  3. (dominated convergence) if \(\left|\xi_n\right| \leq \eta\) a.s. and \(\mathbb {E}\left[\eta \right] < \infty\), then \(\mathbb {E}\left[\xi_n\right] \to \mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right]\).

The question “when can we interchange the limit and the expectation” is not scholastic: on it rest convergence of moments (topic 4), the well-posedness of the definition of characteristic functions (topic 8), and a good half of the proofs of limit theorems. Computing expectations “by definition”, integrating over the abstract \(\Omega\), is impossible — and unnecessary: the next theorem transfers computations to \(\mathbb {R}\).

Theorem 3 (Change of variables theorem) Let \(\xi\) be a random variable, \(g\colon \mathbb {R} \to \mathbb {R}\) a Borel function. Then \[ \mathbb {E}\left[g(\xi )\right] = \int _\Omega g\left(\xi (\omega )\right) \, \mathbb {P}(d\omega ) = \int _{\mathbb {R}} g(x) \, \mathbb {P}_\xi (dx) \] in the sense that if one of the integrals is defined, then so is the other, and they are equal (Shiryaev [@shiryaev2016probability], Ch. 2, §6).

The proof is the “standard machine” of measure theory, worth mastering as an independent technique: the equality is obvious for \(g = \; \mathbb {1}_{B}\) (both sides equal \(\mathbb {P}_\xi (B)\)), extends to simple \(g\) by linearity, to nonnegative \(g\) by the monotone convergence theorem, and to arbitrary \(g\) via \(g = g^+ - g^-\).

In the English-language literature, theorem Theorem 3 is jokingly called the law of the unconscious statistician: whoever applies it usually does not even realize they are passing from an integral over \(\Omega\) to an integral over \(\mathbb {R}\). Now this transition is conscious and legitimate. Practical forms are provided by the notion of a generalized density:

Proposition 4 (Computational formulas) Let \(\mu\) be a \(\sigma\)-finite measure on \((\mathbb {R}, \mathscr {B}\left(\mathbb {R}\right))\) and suppose the distribution \(\mathbb {P}_\xi\) has density with respect to \(\mu\) (Radon-Nikodym derivative) \(f = \frac{d\mathbb {P}_\xi }{d\mu }\). Then \[ \mathbb {E}\left[g(\xi )\right] = \int _{\mathbb {R}} g(x) f(x) \, \mu (dx). \] Special cases:

  1. \(\mu\) is the counting measure on an at most countable set of values: \(f(x_k) = \mathbb {P}\left(\xi = x_k\right)\), and the formula becomes \(\mathbb {E}\left[g(\xi )\right] = \sum_k g(x_k) \mathbb {P}\left(\xi = x_k\right)\);

  2. \(\mu\) is Lebesgue measure: \(f = p_\xi\) is the usual density, and \(\mathbb {E}\left[g(\xi )\right] = \int_{\mathbb {R}} g(x) p_\xi (x) \, dx\);

  3. a mixture: if \(F_\xi = \sum_k \alpha_k F_k\), then \(\mathbb {E}\left[g(\xi )\right] = \sum_k \alpha_k \int_{\mathbb {R}} g \, dF_k\).

The notion of density with respect to a dominating measure is exactly the place where the discrete and continuous worlds are finally glued together: a “distribution table” and a “density” are one and the same object \(\frac{d\mathbb {P}_\xi }{d\mu }\) for different \(\mu\). And this is not mere aesthetics: in statistics (course 3) the likelihood function is exactly the density with respect to a dominating measure, and without the general notion, likelihood for models with mixed distributions cannot even be written down. And mixed distributions are an everyday matter:

ExampleExample 2 - Insurance payout

The annual loss of a client under auto insurance is modeled as follows: with probability \(0{,}9\) there is no insured event (the payout equals \(0\)), and with probability \(0{,}1\) the payout is distributed as \(\operatorname {Exp}(\lambda )\) with \(\frac{1}{\lambda } = 100\) thousand rubles. Find the CDF of the payout \(\xi\), its generalized density, and \(\mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right]\).

SolutionSolution

The CDF is a mixture with an atom at zero: for \(x \geq 0\)

\[ F_\xi (x) = 0{,}9 + 0{,}1 \left(1 - e^{-\lambda x}\right), \qquad F_\xi (x) = 0 \text{ for } x < 0. \]

The distribution is neither discrete nor absolutely continuous. Take the dominating measure \(\mu = \delta_0 + \operatorname {Leb}\) (an atom at zero plus Lebesgue measure); then the generalized density is

\[ f(x) = \frac{d\mathbb {P}_\xi }{d\mu }(x) = \begin{cases} 0{,}9, & x = 0, \\ 0{,}1 \, \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, & x > 0. \end{cases} \]

The expectation, by proposition Proposition 4:

\[ \mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right] = 0 \cdot 0{,}9 + 0{,}1 \int _0^\infty x \, \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \, dx = 0{,}1 \cdot \frac{1}{\lambda } = 10 \text{ thousand rubles.} \]

Notice: there is no special “mixed case” in the theory — a single formula handles everything.

Another formula linking the expectation to the distribution directly is used constantly in the stochastic processes course:

Proposition 5 (The tail formula) For \(\xi \geq 0\) \[ \mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right] = \int _0^\infty \mathbb {P}\left(\xi > t\right) \, dt; \] in particular, for integer-valued \(\xi \geq 0\): \(\mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right] = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \mathbb {P}\left(\xi > n\right)\).

The proof is one line, using Fubini’s theorem (legitimate for nonnegative functions):

\[ \int _0^\infty \mathbb {P}\left(\xi > t\right) \, dt = \int _0^\infty \mathbb {E}\left[\; \mathbb {1}_{\xi > t}\right] \, dt = \mathbb {E}\left[\int _0^\infty \; \mathbb {1}_{t < \xi } \, dt\right] = \mathbb {E}\left[\xi \right]. \]

Each day, independently of the other days, he completes a fraction of the assignment, uniformly distributed on \([0,1]\) (the fractions add up; the assignment is done once the sum of the fractions reaches \(1\)). On average, how many days will completing the assignment take?
