@debt:borel-lebesgue (first half), @debt:measurability (partially) This chapter begins repaying the debts accumulated in the chapters on the probability space–on limit theorems (their complete list is at the end of the chapter on limit theorems). We already defined the abstract notion of a sigma-algebra back in week 1, when we needed it for geometric probability. Here we will complete the full picture around it: simpler and more visual systems of sets — semirings, rings, algebras — from which it is convenient to generate sigma-algebras, and, most importantly, we will finally give a rigorous definition of a specific, canonical sigma-algebra \(\mathscr {B}\left(\mathbb {R}^{n}\right)\) — the Borel sigma-algebra — which we have been using in geometric models and in the definition of a random variable while knowing only its name. The second half of debt @debt:borel-lebesgue — Lebesgue measure — will be repaid in the chapters on Carathéodory’s theorem–on Lebesgue measure.

Measure theory is concerned with computing lengths, areas, volumes, and so on. All these numerical characteristics — length (of a linear set), area (of a planar set), and volume (of a set in three-dimensional space) — are known as measures, defined on (suitable) subsets of an ambient space, usually denoted \(\Omega\). In the examples above, \(\Omega\) equals the one-, two-, or three-dimensional space \(\mathbb {R}^{1}, \mathbb {R}^{2}, \mathbb {R}^{3}\), respectively.

The advantage of this abstraction is that the notion of measure can be applied to a wide range of situations, from discrete spaces (the familiar high-school models with a finite or countable number of possible outcomes of a random experiment) to finite-dimensional spaces \(\mathbb {R}^n, n \geq 1\), where the famous Lebesgue measure is \(n\)-dimensional volume (in particular, this is length for \(n=1\), area for \(n=2\), ordinary volume for \(n=3\)), and even to infinite-dimensional spaces (such as \(C[0,1]\) or \(C[0, \infty )\), where, for instance, Wiener measure of the corresponding set of functions is the probability that a random trajectory undergoing Brownian motion belongs to that set).

To begin, let us consider the natural choice of the sample space \(\Omega\) when we need to address probabilistic questions concerning simple experiments with various possible outcomes.

  • Experiment 1: Toss a fair coin twice and record the outcome of each toss. The collection of all (four) possible outcomes can be described as \(\Omega = \{ H H, H T, T H, T T\}\), where \(H\) is heads and \(T\) is tails.

  • Experiment 2: Roll a fair six-sided die and record the outcome. We know that there are six possible outcomes: \(\Omega =\{ 1,2,3,4,5,6\}\).

  • Experiment 3: Toss a fair coin until heads first appears, and record the number of tosses. The outcomes of this experiment can be parametrized by the set \(\Omega =\mathbb {N}\) of natural numbers.

  • Experiment 4: Choose a random point “uniformly distributed on \(I=(0,1]\)” and record the outcome. Here the sample space is \(\Omega =I\).

More general experiments will lead to various sets of outcomes \(\Omega\). Given an experiment with the set of all outcomes denoted \(\Omega\), one is interested in specific events. For example,

  • in experiment 1, let \(E_1\) be the event \(\{\) the first toss comes up \(H\}\);

  • in experiment 2, let \(E_2\) be the event \(\{\) the outcome is an even number \(\}\);

  • in experiment 3, let \(E_3\) be the event \(\{\) heads appears in at most 10 tosses,

  • in experiment 4, let \(E_4\) be the event \(\{\) the chosen number is greater than one half \(\}\).

As seen above, each such event is typically a subset of \(\Omega\). In experiments 1, 2, and 3, we were able to enumerate the set of outcomes, so we could take the set of all subsets of \(\Omega\), i.e., the set of all events, equal to the power set \(\mathcal{P}(\Omega )\). However, when the set \(\Omega\) is infinite, as in experiment 4, a problem arises in defining the probability of arbitrary subsets of \(\Omega\). To understand precisely the nature of this problem, consider the following question.

Question: given a set \(A \subset \mathbb {R}^3\), can we measure the volume of \(A\)?

Let us examine the “reasonable requirements” that a procedure for assigning volumes to sets should satisfy. In particular, if \(|S|\) denotes the volume of a set \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\), then it seems natural to require that the assignment \(S \mapsto |S|\) satisfy the following conditions:

  1. \(|S| \in [0, \infty ]\). (Volumes cannot be negative.)

  2. \(\left|S_1 \cup S_2\right|=\left|S_1\right|+\left|S_2\right|\), if \(S_1 \cap S_2=\emptyset\). (Volumes of disjoint sets should “add up.”)

  3. If \(\left\{ S_n\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty }\) is an increasing sequence of sets with union \(S\), i.e., \(S_1 \subset S_2 \subset \ldots\) and \(S=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty } S_n=S\), then \(|S|=\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty }\left|S_n\right|\). (This is a “continuity requirement,” which seems natural.)

  4. \(|C|=1\), if \(C = [0,1]^n\) is the \(n\)-dimensional cube with side \(1\).

  5. If \(x \in \mathbb {R}^3\) and \(S+x=\{ s+x: s \in S\}\) denotes the translate of \(S\) by \(x\), then \(|S+x|=|S|\): (volumes remain invariant under translation.)

Unfortunately, it is impossible to define \(|S|\) for all subsets \(S\) of \(\mathbb {R}^3\) in such a way that all the conditions (1)–(5) above are satisfied. This problem is caused by the existence of certain very strange “nonmeasurable” sets.

Consequently, whether the motivation comes from computing the probabilities of certain events of an experiment with sample space \(\Omega\), or from computing the lengths of subsets of a space \(\Omega\), one must first come to grips with the notion of events or subsets. This is exactly what we do in the next section.

Why do we need systems of sets?

  • As already noted, it is impossible (and, for \(\mathbb {R}^n\), \(n \ge 1\), simply contradictory within ZFC with the axiom of choice) to define a measure on all subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) so that it is countably additive, translation-invariant, and consistent with the ordinary length/area/volume — nonmeasurable sets exist (for example, the Vitali set). Therefore, measure must be defined not on all sets, but on a specially chosen class of sets.

  • This class must be sufficiently rich to include all the “reasonable” sets encountered in practice (intervals, open and closed sets, countable unions and intersections, etc.), and sufficiently structured (closed under complements, countable unions, and countable intersections) so that operations on measurable sets and passages to the limit do not take us outside the class.

  • It is precisely this structure that the notion of a \(\sigma\)-algebra possesses. In probability theory, a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\) of events plays the same role: it specifies the class of sets (events) for which probability is defined at all, and guarantees that countable combinations of events (unions, intersections, complements) are themselves events.

  • It is convenient to build \(\sigma\)-algebras not “by hand,” but by generating them from simpler and more visual systems of sets (semirings, rings, algebras) — via the operation “the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing a given class of sets.” This is how, in particular, the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb {R}^n)\) arises — the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by the open sets (or, equivalently, by the half-open intervals of the form \((x,y]\)) in \(\mathbb {R}^n\).

  • The study of various systems of sets (semiring, ring, algebra, \(\sigma\)-algebra, monotone class) and the relationships between them is the necessary technical foundation on which both Lebesgue measure (via Carathéodory’s theorem) and the general theory of probability measures are subsequently built.

1 Semirings, rings, algebras, sigma-algebras

Throughout what follows, \(\mathcal{S}\) is some family of subsets of a set \(\Omega\), i.e., \(\mathcal{S} \subset 2^\Omega\).

Definition 1 A family of subsets \(\mathcal{S}\) is called a semiring if

  1. \(\varnothing \in \mathcal{S}\);

  2. If \(A, B \in \mathcal{S}\), then \(A \cap B \in \mathcal{S}\) (closure under intersection);

  3. If \(A, B \in \mathcal{S}, B \subset A\), then there exists in \(\mathcal{S}\) a finite collection of disjoint sets \(C_1, \dots , C_n\) (also disjoint from \(B\)) such that

    \[ \begin{align} A = B \sqcup C_1 \sqcup \dots \sqcup C_n \end{align} \]

The most important semiring for us will be the family of half-open intervals in \(\mathbb {R}^{n}\):

\[ \mathcal{S} = \left\{ (x,y] \; \; : \; \; x,y \in \mathbb {R}^{n}\right\} \]

where

\[ (x,y] = (x_1, y_1] \times \ldots \times (x_n, y_n] \]

Condition 3 of a semiring, illustrated with the semiring of (multidimensional, here 2-dimensional) half-open intervals. Note the boundaries of the sets \(B,C_1,\ldots,C_4\) — hover over a region to see its exact definition. Since we used half-open intervals, the union \(A = B \sqcup C_1 \sqcup C_2 \sqcup C_3 \sqcup C_4\) is indeed disjoint.

Definition 2 If a semiring \(\mathcal{S} \subset 2^{\Omega }\) is additionally closed under unions (i.e., \(A,B \in \mathcal{S} \; \Rightarrow \; A \cup B \in \mathcal{S}\)), then it is called a ring.

A ring can be obtained from a semiring simply by taking all possible finite unions of elements of the semiring. In particular, the system of sets each of which can be represented as a finite union of half-open intervals is itself a semiring.

Definition 3 If \(\mathcal{S}\) is a ring, and additionally \(\Omega \in \mathcal{S}\), then the ring \(\mathcal{S}\) is called an algebra.

Definition 4 \(\mathcal{S}\) is a sigma-ring (abbr. \(\sigma\)-ring) if \(\mathcal{S}\) is a ring and \(\mathcal{S}\) is closed under countable unions, i.e., \(\forall (A_i)_{i=1}^\infty \subset \mathcal{S}\) we have \[ \begin{align} \bigcup _{i=1}^{\infty } A_i \in \mathcal{S} \end{align} \] A sigma-algebra (abbr. \(\sigma\)-algebra) is defined analogously.

2 The minimal sigma-algebra, the minimal system

Definition 5 Let \(\mathcal{D}\) be an arbitrary family of subsets of \(\Omega\): \(\mathcal{D} \subset 2^{\Omega }\). Then the minimal \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by \(\mathcal{D}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{S}\) that is contained in every other sigma-algebra containing \(\mathcal{D}\). Notation: \(\sigma (\mathcal{D})\). One says that \(\mathcal{D}\) generates \(\sigma (\mathcal{D})\).

The minimal algebra, minimal ring, minimal \(\lambda\)-system (see the definition below) (notation \(\lambda (\mathcal{D})\)), and minimal monotone class (definition below) (notation: \(M(\mathcal{D})\)) are defined analogously.

ExampleExample 1

Prove that \(\forall \Omega , \; \forall \mathcal{D} \subset 2^\Omega\) the minimal \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma (\mathcal{D})\) exists and is unique.

SolutionSolution

It is easy to show that the intersection of any collection of sigma-algebras (even an uncountable one) is again a sigma-algebra. Hence \(\sigma (\mathcal{D})\) is \[ \sigma (\mathcal{D}) = \bigcap _{\substack {\mathcal{F} \text{ is a } \sigma \text{-alg.}\\ \mathcal{F} \supset \mathcal{D}}} \mathcal{F} \tag{1}\] Note that the intersection on the right is nonempty, since \(2^\Omega\) is a sigma-algebra and \(2^\Omega \supset \mathcal{D}\) by assumption. QED.

ExampleExample 2

Describe the \(\sigma\)-algebra of subsets of the real line \(\mathbb {R}\) generated by the system of singletons: \[ \mathcal{D} = \left\{ \left\{ x\right\} \mid x \in \mathbb {R}\right\} \]

SolutionSolution

In practice, obtaining the minimal sigma-algebra directly via formula Equation 1 is difficult: too many sigma-algebras would need to be examined. It is much more convenient to work “bottom-up”: using the operations under which a sigma-algebra is closed, build new subsets of \(\Omega\) from those already in \(\mathcal{D}\). Then, from the old and the newly constructed sets, build still more, and so on. At some step new sets will stop appearing, closure under the operations will be reached, and the resulting collection will be the minimal sigma-algebra. The number of steps required may be finite (the simple situation) or infinite.

So, we take finite and countable unions of singletons, obtaining the at-most-countable subsets of \(\mathbb {R}\). Next we take complements, obtaining

\[ \mathcal{F} = \left\{ A \subset \mathbb {R} \mid \text{ either } A \text{, or } A^\complement \text{ is at most countable}\right\} \]

It is straightforward to check that the axioms of a sigma-algebra applied to \(\mathcal{F}\) yield no new subsets, so \(\mathcal{F}\) is a sigma-algebra, and moreover \(\mathcal{F} = \sigma (\mathcal{D})\).

Definition 6 Let a topological space \((X,\tau )\)1 be given. The sigma-algebra \(\sigma (\tau )\) is called the Borel sigma-algebra and is denoted \(\mathscr {B}\left(X\right)\). It depends on \(\tau\), but as a rule it is clear from context which topology \(\tau\) is being considered, and so \(\tau\) is not indicated in the notation.

Briefly: the Borel sigma-algebra is the minimal sigma-algebra generated by the open subsets.

Footnotes

  1. Recall that a collection of subsets \(\tau \subset 2^X\) is called a topology on \(X\) if

    1. \(\varnothing , X \in \tau\);

    2. \(\tau\) is closed under finite intersections;

    3. \(\tau\) is closed under arbitrary, even uncountable, unions. The elements of \(\tau\) are called the open subsets of \(X\). A subcollection \(\tau ' \subset \tau\) is called a base for the topology \(\tau\) if every element of \(\tau\) can be represented as a union of some number of elements of \(\tau '\). In \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (more generally, in any metric space), it is customary to take the open balls as a base for the topology, i.e., sets of the form \(\left\{ x \in X \mid \rho (x, x_0) < \varepsilon \right\}\), \(x_0 \in X\), \(\varepsilon > 0\).
