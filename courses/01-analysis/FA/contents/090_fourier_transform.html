1 The Fourier transform on \(L^{1}(\mathbb {R})\)

In this section \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\).

Let \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\). The Fourier transform of \(f\) is a new function from \(\mathbb {R}\) to \(\mathbb {C}\), defined by two similar methods:

\[ (\mathcal{F}_1f)(\omega ) = \widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) := \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x) \cdot e^{-i\omega x} \; dx \]

This convention is more common in the Russian-language literature.

\[ (\mathcal{F}_2f)(t) = \widehat{f}^{2}(t) := \int _{\mathbb {R}} f(x) \cdot e^{-2\pi i tx} \; dx \]

This convention is more common in the English-language literature.

The inverse Fourier transform of a function \(h \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\) is defined as:

\[ (\mathcal{F}^{-1}_1 h)(x) := \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \int _{\mathbb {R}} h(\omega ) \cdot e^{i\omega x} \; d\omega \]

\[ (\mathcal{F}^{-1}_2 h)(x) := \int _{\mathbb {R}} h(t) \cdot e^{2 \pi i t x} \; dt \]

For now we only define the inverse Fourier transform formally. We do not claim that the Fourier transform is invertible on \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\). In general this is false: as we will see below, the image of the Fourier transform on \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\) falls outside \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\). That is, there exists a function in \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\) whose Fourier transform turns out not to be integrable. However, on certain subspaces of \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\) — for example, on the space of Schwartz test functions \(\mathcal{S}\) — the Fourier transform \(\mathcal{F}_{1,2}\) will be invertible (in particular, the image will not fall outside the space itself), and the inverse will be given by \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{1,2}\) defined above.

Let us immediately discuss the relationship between these two ways of defining the Fourier transform:

\[ \widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \cdot \widehat{f}^{2}\left(\frac{\omega }{2\pi }\right), \qquad \widehat{f}^{2}(t) = \sqrt{2\pi } \cdot \widehat{f}^{1}(2\pi t) \]

In terms of operators: if we define the operator \(\operatorname {DIL}_{b}\) by \((\operatorname {DIL}_b f)(x) = f(bx)\), then

\[ \begin{align} \mathcal{F}_1 & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \cdot \mathcal{F}_2 \cdot \operatorname {DIL}_{1/2\pi } \\ \mathcal{F}_2 & = \sqrt{2\pi } \cdot \mathcal{F}_1 \cdot \operatorname {DIL}_{2\pi } \end{align} \]

In what follows, when the notation carries no index, the computation applies to both ways of defining the FT.

Enum-item(2)

\[ \begin{align} \widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }}\int _{0}^{+\infty }e^{-x - i\omega x}dx + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \int _{-\infty }^{0}e^{x - i\omega x}dx =\\ & = +\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }}\frac{1}{1 + i\omega } + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }}\frac{1}{1 - i\omega } = \frac{\sqrt{2/\pi }}{1 + \omega ^2} \end{align} \]

For the right-hand column it is natural to take \(f(x) = e^{-2\pi \left|x\right|}\) (the analogue of \(e^{-\left|x\right|}\), consistent with the factor \(2\pi\) in \(\mathcal F_2\)): \[ \begin{align} \widehat{f}^{2}(t) & = \int _{0}^{+\infty }e^{-2\pi x - 2\pi i tx}dx + \int _{-\infty }^{0}e^{2\pi x - 2\pi i tx}dx = \\ & = \frac{1}{2\pi }\frac{1}{1 + it} + \frac{1}{2\pi }\frac{1}{1 - it} = \frac{1}{\pi (1+t^2)} \end{align} \] (used in example Example 3 below; also verified numerically.)

Lemma 1 The Fourier transform is a linear map on \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\).

\[ f(x) = x \]

\[ f(x) = x^2 \]

Lemma 2 (Riemann–Lebesgue) Let \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\). Then \(\mathcal{F}_{1,2}f \in C_0(\mathbb {R})\), and moreover

\[ \left\| \widehat{f}^{1}\right\| _{\infty } \leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \left\| f\right\| _{1} \]

\[ \left\| \widehat{f}^{2}\right\| _{\infty } \leq \left\| f\right\| _{1} \]

In short: \(\mathcal{F}: L^1(\mathbb {R}) \to C_0(\mathbb {R})\) is a bounded linear map, with

\[ \left\| \mathcal{F}_1\right\| \leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \]

\[ \left\| \mathcal{F}_2\right\| \leq 1 \]

Recall that \(C_0(\mathbb {R})\) is the space of continuous functions on \(\mathbb {R}\) that tend to zero at infinity: \(f(x) \xrightarrow [x \to \infty ]{} 0\). Together with the norm \(\left\| f\right\|_{\infty } = \sup_{x \in \mathbb {R}} \left|f\right|\) it forms a Banach space, more precisely a Banach subspace of \(L^\infty (\mathbb {R})\). Moreover, if \(f\in C_0(\mathbb {R})\), then \(f\) is uniformly continuous. That is, the Fourier transform of a function in \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\) is necessarily a uniformly continuous function.

Lemma 3 (Derivative of the Fourier transform) Let \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\). Suppose that \(xf(x) =: g(x)\) is also an integrable function: \(g \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\). Then the Fourier transform is continuously differentiable on all of \(\mathbb {R}\), with

\[ \left(\widehat{f}^{1}\right)' = -i \cdot \widehat{g}^{1} \]

\[ \left(\widehat{f}^{2}\right)' = - 2\pi i \cdot \widehat{g}^{2} \]

In terms of maps: denote by \(M\) multiplication by the identity function, \((Mf)(x) = x\cdot f(x)\). Then if \(f, Mf \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\), we have \(\mathcal{F}_{1,2}f \in C^1(\mathbb {R})\), and for the function \(f\) we have

\[ D\mathcal{F}_1 = -i \cdot \mathcal{F}_1M \]

\[ D\mathcal{F}_2 = -2\pi i \cdot \mathcal{F}_2M \]

where \(Df := f'\) is the differentiation operator.

Lemma 4 (Fourier transform of a derivative) Let \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\) be a continuously differentiable function whose derivative is also integrable: \(f' \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\). Then

\[ \widehat{f'}^{1} = i t \cdot \widehat{f}^{1} \]

\[ \widehat{f'}^{2} = 2\pi i t \cdot \widehat{f}^{2} \]

Among other things, this means that \(\widehat{f}^{1,2}(y) = o(1/y)\) as \(y \to \infty\).

In terms of maps: if \(f \in C^1(\mathbb {R})\), \(Df \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\), then for the function \(f\) we have

\[ \mathcal{F}_1D =i \cdot M\mathcal{F}_1 \]

\[ \mathcal{F}_2D = 2\pi i \cdot M\mathcal{F}_2 \]
ExampleExample 2

Let

\[ f(x) = e^{-x^2/2} \]

\[ f(x) = e^{-\pi x^2} \]

Find the Fourier transform of \(f\).

SolutionSolution

  • Method 1: direct computation. We know the Gaussian integral: \(\int_{-\infty }^{+\infty }e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt{\pi }\). Then

    \[ \begin{align} \widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } e^{- x^2/2} \cdot e^{-i \omega x} \; dx = \\ & = \frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\omega ^2}}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \; \cdot \; \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(x + i\omega \right)^2} \; dx = \\ & = \frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\omega ^2}\cdot \sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \cdot \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } e^{-\frac{1}{2} \left(x + it\right)^2} \; d\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(x + it\right)\right) = \\ & = e^{-\frac{1}{2}\omega ^2} \end{align} \]

    \[ \begin{align} \widehat{f}^{2}(t) & = \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } e^{-\pi x^2} \cdot e^{-2\pi i t x} \; dx = \\ & = e^{-\pi t^2} \; \cdot \; \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } e^{-\pi \left(x + it\right)^2} \; dx = \\ & = e^{-\pi t^2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi }} \cdot \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } e^{-\pi \left(x + it\right)^2} \; d\left(\sqrt{\pi }\left(x + it\right)\right) = \\ & = e^{-\pi t^2} \end{align} \]

  • Method 2: via derivatives. We know that \(g(x) = xe^{-\pi x^2}\) is integrable on all of \(\mathbb {R}\). Hence

    \[ \begin{align} \left(\widehat{f}^{2}\right)'(t) & = - 2\pi i \widehat{g}^{2}(t) = -2\pi i \cdot \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty }xe^{-\pi x^2} \cdot e^{-2\pi i t x} \; dx = \\ & = \underbrace{\left(ie^{-\pi x^2} \cdot e^{-2\pi i t x}\right)\bigg\rvert _{x=-\infty }^{x=+\infty }}_{=0} \quad - \quad 2\pi t \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } e^{-\pi x^2} \cdot e^{-2\pi i t x} \; dx = \\ & = -2\pi t \widehat{f}^{2}(t) \end{align} \]

    for every \(t \in \mathbb {R}\). Moreover, \(f'(t) = -2\pi i e^{\pi t^2} = -2\pi t f(t)\). Hence,

    \[ \left(\frac{\widehat{f}^{2}}{f}\right)'(t) = \frac{f(t) \cdot \left(\widehat{f}^{2}\right)'(t) - f'(t) \cdot \widehat{f}^{2}(t)}{f^2(t)} = -2\pi t \frac{f(t) \widehat{f}^{2}(t) - f(t) \widehat{f}^{2}(t)}{f^2(t)} = 0 \]

    for all \(t \in \mathbb {R}\). Hence \(\frac{\widehat{f}^{2}}{f}\) is a constant: there exists \(c\in \mathbb {C}\) such that \(\frac{\widehat{f}^{2}(t)}{f(t)} = c\) for all \(t \in \mathbb {R}\). That is, \(\widehat{f}^{2}(t) = cf(t)\).

    Let us find \(c\):

    \[ \widehat{f}^{2}(0) = \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } e^{-\pi x^2} \; dx = f(0) \]

    Hence \(c = 1\).

AnswerAnswer

\(\widehat{f} = f\).

Lemma 5 (The Fourier transform versus translation, modulation, and dilation) Let \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\), \(b \in \mathbb {R}\), \(t \in \mathbb {R}\). Then

  • If \(g(x) := f(x - b)\), then

    \[ \widehat{g}^{1}(t) = e^{-i b t} \cdot \widehat{f}^{1}(t) \]

    \[ \widehat{g}^{2}(t) = e^{-2\pi i b t} \cdot \widehat{f}^{2}(t) \]

  • If \(g(x) := e^{i b x} \cdot f(x)\), then \[ \widehat{g}^{1}(t) =\widehat{f}^{1}(t - b) \]

    If \(g(x) := e^{2\pi i b x} \cdot f(x)\), then \[ \widehat{g}^{2}(t) =\widehat{f}^{2}(t - b) \]

  • If \(b \neq 0\) and \(g(x) := f(bx)\), then

    \[ \widehat{g}^{1}(\omega ) =\frac{1}{\left|b\right|}\widehat{f}^{1}\left(\frac{\omega }{b}\right) \]

    \[ \widehat{g}^{2}(t) =\frac{1}{\left|b\right|}\widehat{f}^{2}\left(\frac{t}{b}\right) \]

Then \[ \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } \widehat{f}(t) \cdot g(t) \; dt = \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } f(t) \cdot \widehat{g}(t) \; dt = \tag{2}\] (this formula holds for both ways of defining the Fourier transform).

2 Convolution on \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\)

Our goal is to prove the Fourier inversion formula: if \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\) and \(\mathcal{F}_{1,2}{f} \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\), then

\[ f = \mathcal{F}_{1}^{-1} \mathcal{F}_{1} f \]

\[ f = \mathcal{F}_{2}^{-1} \mathcal{F}_{2} f \tag{3}\]

for almost every \(x \in \mathbb {R}\). In other words,

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }}\int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } \widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) \cdot e^{i x \omega } d\omega \]

\[ f(x) = \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } \widehat{f}^{2}(t) \cdot e^{2\pi i x t} dt \]

To understand this equality, fix \(x\) and try to express the right-hand side in terms of \(f\). Direct application of formula Equation 2 is impossible, since the function

\[ \omega \mapsto e^{ix\omega } \]

\[ t \mapsto e^{2\pi ixt} \]

does not lie in \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\). We therefore introduce a smoothing factor: for fixed \(y>0\), consider

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }}\int _{-\infty }^{\infty }\widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) \cdot \underbrace{e^{-y|\omega |} \cdot e^{ix\omega }}_{=: h(t)}\; d\omega \enclose {circle}{=} \]

\[ \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }\widehat{f}^{2}(t) \cdot \underbrace{e^{-2\pi y|t|} \cdot e^{2\pi ixt}}_{=: h(t)}\; dt \enclose {circle}{=} \tag{4}\]

Here \(t \mapsto e^{-y|t|}\) (resp. \(t \mapsto e^{-2\pi y|t|}\)) and \(h\) itself lie in \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\), and as \(y \downarrow 0\) we have

\[ e^{-y|\omega |} \xrightarrow []{} 1 \quad \text{ and } \quad h(\omega ) \xrightarrow []{} e^{i x \omega } \]

\[ e^{-2\pi y|t|} \xrightarrow []{} 1 \quad \text{ and } \quad h(t) \xrightarrow []{} e^{2\pi i x t} \]

That is, formula Equation 4 is a good approximation of the right-hand side in Equation 3. Let us expand:

\[ \begin{align} & \enclose {circle}{=} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }}\int _{-\infty }^{\infty } \widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) \cdot h(\omega ) \; d\omega = \\ & \text{ (using formula \ref{FourierTransform:DualityFormula})}\\ & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }}\int _{-\infty }^{\infty }f(\omega ) \cdot \widehat{h}^{1}(\omega ) = \\ & \text{ (using example \ref{FourierTransform:ExpRotatedFourierTr})}\\ & = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }f(\omega ) \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{\pi }\frac{y}{(x-\omega )^2 + y^2}}_{=:P_y(x-\omega )} \; d\omega \\ & = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }f(\omega ) \cdot P_y(x-\omega ) \; d\omega \end{align} \]

\[ \begin{align} & \enclose {circle}{=} \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } \widehat{f}^{2}(t) \cdot h(t) \; dt = \\ & \text{ (using formula \ref{FourierTransform:DualityFormula})}\\ & = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }f(t) \cdot \widehat{h}^{2}(t) = \\ & \text{ (using example \ref{FourierTransform:ExpRotatedFourierTr})}\\ & = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }f(t) \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{\pi }\frac{y}{(x-t)^2 + y^2}}_{=:P_y(x-t)} \; dt \\ & = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }f(t) \cdot P_y(x-t) \; dt \end{align} \]

The function \(P_y: \mathbb {R} \to (0,+\infty )\), defined by \(P_y(x) = \frac{1}{\pi }\frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}\), is called the Poisson kernel. The formula obtained leads us to study integrals of the form

\[ \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } f(t) \cdot g(x-t)\; dt, \]

that is, convolutions.

Definition 1 Let \(f,g : \mathbb {R} \to \mathbb {C}\) be measurable functions. The convolution of \(f\) and \(g\) is the function \[ \left(f \ast g\right)(x) := \int _{-\infty }^{+\infty } f(t) \cdot g(x-t) \; dt \]

Lemma 7 If \(f, g \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\), then \(f \ast g\) is defined for almost every \(x \in \mathbb {R}\), \(f \ast g \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\), and \[ \left\| f \ast g\right\| _{1} \leq \left\| f\right\| _{1} \cdot \left\| g\right\| _{1} \]

If \(p \in (1, \infty ]\), then neither \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\) nor \(L^p(\mathbb {R})\) is a subset of the other. Thus we do not yet know whether the expression \(f * g\) makes sense for \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\) and \(g \in L^p(\mathbb {R})\). However, the following result, a strengthening of the previous lemma, shows that everything works out.

Lemma 8 (The \(L^p\)-norm of a convolution) Let \(1\leq p \leq \infty\), \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\), \(g \in L^p(\mathbb {R})\). Then \((f \ast g)(x)\) is defined for almost every \(x \in \mathbb {R}\) (with respect to Lebesgue measure), and

\[ \left\| f \ast g\right\| _{p} \leq \left\| f\right\| _{1} \cdot \left\| g\right\| _{p} \]

Lemma 9 (Convolution is commutative) Let \(f,g : \mathbb {R} \to \mathbb {C}\) be measurable functions, \(x \in \mathbb {R}\), and suppose \((f \ast g)(x)\) is defined. Then \[ (f \ast g)(x) = (g \ast f)(x) \]

Lemma 10 (Convolution versus the Fourier transform) Let \(f,g \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\). Then

\[ \widehat{f \ast g}^{1} = \sqrt{2\pi } \cdot \widehat{f}^{1} \cdot \widehat{g}^{1} \]

\[ \widehat{f \ast g}^{2} = \widehat{f}^{2} \cdot \widehat{g}^{2} \]

3 The inverse Fourier transform

Theorem 1 If \(f, \widehat{f} \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\), then \[ f = \mathcal{F}^{-1}f \] Equivalently: \[ f(x) = \widehat{\widehat{f}}(-x) \]

Lemma 11 A function in \(L^1\) can be uniquely recovered from its Fourier transform. In other words, \(\mathcal{F}: L^1(\mathbb {R}) \to C_{0}(\mathbb {R})\) is an injective map.

ExampleExample 5

Prove the injectivity of \(\mathcal{F}\).

SolutionSolution

Since \(\mathcal{F}\) is a linear map, it suffices to prove that no function other than zero maps to zero. Suppose \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\) and \(\widehat{f} \equiv 0\). But then \(\widehat{f} \in L^1\) and the inverse transform applies: \(f = \mathcal{F}^{-1}f = 0\).

ExampleExample 6

Prove that convolution is associative: \[ \left(f \ast g\right) \ast h = f \ast \left(g \ast h\right) \] for all \(f,g,h \in L^1(\mathbb {R})\).

SolutionSolution

This result could be proved directly, using the definition of convolution together with the Tonelli/Fubini theorems. However, the following elegant proof deserves attention.

The Fourier transform turns the left-hand and right-hand sides into

\[ 2\pi \cdot \widehat{f}^{1} \cdot \widehat{g}^{1} \cdot \widehat{h}^{1} \]

\[ \widehat{f}^{2} \cdot \widehat{g}^{2} \cdot \widehat{h}^{2} \]

(we no longer include parentheses, since ordinary multiplication is associative). Moreover, the Fourier transform is an injective map. Hence the original elements are equal as well.

4 The Fourier transform on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\)

Recall that neither \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\) embeds into \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\), nor conversely.

Theorem 2 (Plancherel; the FT preserves the 2-norm) Let \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R}) \cap L^2(\mathbb {R})\). Then \[ \left\| f\right\| _{2} = \left\| \mathcal{F}f\right\| _{2} \]

Note that \(L^1(\mathbb {R}) \cap L^2(\mathbb {R})\) is everywhere dense in \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\). Indeed, let \(f \in L^2(\mathbb {R})\) be an arbitrary element of \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\), and let \(f_n := f \cdot \; \mathbb {1}_{[-n,n]}\). Then the restriction of \(f_n\) to \([-n,n]\) lies in \(L^2[-n,n]\), and hence also in \(L^1[-n,n]\), so \(f_n \in L^1(\mathbb {R}) \cap L^2(\mathbb {R})\). Moreover,

\[ f_n \xrightarrow [n \to \infty ]{L^2(\mathbb {R})} f \]

Indeed, let \(g_n = \left|f - f_n\right|^2 = \left|f\right|^2 \cdot \; \mathbb {1}_{[-n,n]^{\complement }}\). Then \(g_n \geq 0\) for all \(n\), \(g_n\) decreases monotonically pointwise to zero, and \(\left|g_n\right| \leq \left|f\right|^2\) for all \(n\), where \(\left|f\right|^2\) is integrable. Then, by the dominated convergence theorem, \(g_n \xrightarrow [n \to \infty ]{L^1(\mathbb {R})} 0\).

Definition 2 The Fourier transform \(\mathcal{F}\) on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\) is the continuous extension of the FT from \(L^1(\mathbb {R}) \cap L^2(\mathbb {R})\) to \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\).

The inverse Fourier transform \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\) is defined analogously.

Suppose \(f \in L^2(\mathbb {R})\) and \(f \not\in L^1(\mathbb {R})\). One should not mistakenly assume that

\[ \widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }}\int _{-\infty }^{\infty } f(x) e^{-i\omega x} d x \]

\[ \widehat{f}^{2}(t) = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } f(x) e^{-2 \pi i t x} d x \]

Indeed, the integral above is meaningless, since the modulus of the function under the integral coincides with \(\left|f\right|\) and \(f \notin L^1(\mathbb {R})\). Instead of defining \(\mathcal{F} f\) via the equation above, \(\mathcal{F} f\) must be defined as the limit in \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\) of the sequence \(\mathcal{F}f_1, \mathcal{F}f_2, \ldots\), where \(f_1, f_2, \ldots\) is a sequence in \(L^1(\mathbb {R}) \cap L^2(\mathbb {R})\) such that

\[ \left\| f - f_n\right\| _{2} \xrightarrow [n \to \infty ]{} 0 \]

For example, one can take \(f_n=f \cdot \; \mathbb {1}_{[-n,n]}\). That is, the FT for \(f \in L^2(\mathbb {R})\) can be defined as

\[ \widehat{f}^{1}(\omega ) = \lim _{n \to +\infty }\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }} \int _{-n}^{n} f(x) \cdot e^{-i\omega x} \; dx \]

\[ \widehat{f}^{2}(t) = \lim _{n \to +\infty } \int _{-n}^{n} f(x) \cdot e^{-2\pi it x} \; dx \]

Since \(\mathcal{F}\) is obtained by continuously extending [in the norm of \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\)] the Fourier transform from \(L^1(\mathbb {R}) \cap L^2(\mathbb {R})\) to \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\), we know that \(\| \mathcal{F} f\|_2=\| f\|_2\) for all \(f \in L^2(\mathbb {R})\). In other words, \(\mathcal{F}\) is an isometry on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\). The following result shows that a much stronger statement holds: \(\mathcal{F}\) is a unitary operator.

Theorem 3 (Properties of the FT on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\)) Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the FT on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\).

  1. \(\mathcal{F}\) is a unitary operator on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\). In more detail:

    • The image of \(\mathcal{F}\) on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\) lies entirely in \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\), i.e., \(\mathcal{F}: L^2(\mathbb {R}) \to L^2(\mathbb {R})\);

    • \(\mathcal{F}\) is a bounded linear operator on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\);

    • \(\mathcal{F}\) is invertible, and its inverse is the inverse Fourier transform \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\);

    • \(\mathcal{F}^{-1} = \mathcal{F}^{*}\), i.e., \(\mathcal{F}^{*}\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}^{*} = I\).

  2. \(\mathcal{F}^2 = \mathcal{R}\), where \((\mathcal{R}f)(x) := f(-x)\) is the operator reflecting a function about the vertical axis. As a consequence we obtain \(\mathcal{F}^{4} = I\).

  3. \(\sigma (\mathcal{F}) = \left\{ 1,i, -1, -i\right\}\).

5 The Fourier transform on \(\mathcal{S}\) and on \(\mathcal{S}'\)

By definition \(f \in \mathcal{S}\) if \(f\) is infinitely continuously differentiable, and \(f\) together with every derivative of it decays at infinity to zero faster than any polynomial. This is equivalent to saying that \(f\) is infinitely differentiable and all of its seminorms are finite:

\[ p_{n,k}(f) := \sup _{t \in \mathbb {R}} \left|t^n \cdot f^{(k)}(t)\right|, \qquad n,k \in \mathbb {Z}_+ = \left\{ 0,1,2,\ldots \right\} \]

In terms of operators:

\[ p_{n,k}(f) = \left\| M^n D^k f\right\| _{\infty } \]

where \((Mf)(t) = t \cdot f(t)\), \(Df = f'\).

It is easy to show that \(\mathcal{S}\) is contained in both \(L^1(\mathbb {R})\) and \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\).
