1 Vector spaces

1.1 Vector spaces and subspaces

\(\mathbb {F}\) denotes either \(\mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\).

A vector space \(V\) over a field \(\mathbb {F}\) is …

Recall that a subset \(U\) of a vector space \(V\) is a subspace of \(V\) if and only if \(U\) satisfies the following three conditions:

  1. zero element: \(0 \in U\);

  2. closure under addition: \(f, g \in U \implies f+g \in U\);

  3. closure under scalar multiplication: \(\alpha \in \mathbb {F}, f \in U \implies \alpha f \in U\).

These three conditions usually allow one to quickly determine whether a given subset of \(V\) is a subspace of \(V\), as illustrated below. All the examples below except the first item are related to the notion of measure.

2 Normed vector spaces

2.1 Norms and complete norms

Let \(V\) be a vector space over a field \(\mathbb {F}\) (\(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\)). A function \(\left\| \cdot \right\| : V \to [0, +\infty )\) is called a norm if it is positive definite, absolutely homogeneous, and subadditive (i.e., it satisfies the triangle inequality; see the detailed definition above). A vector space \(V\) equipped with a norm is called a normed space (NS).

ExampleExample 1

Determine whether the following functions are norms on the corresponding vector spaces.

  1. \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_1\) and \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_{\infty }\) on \(\mathbb {F}^n\);

  2. \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_1\) on \(\ell^1\);

  3. Let \(X \neq \varnothing\). Let \(b(X)\) be the set of bounded functions on \(X\). For \(f \in b(X)\) define

\[ \left\| f\right\| := \sup _{x \in X}\left|f(x)\right| \]

  1. On \(C[0,1]\) define

\[ \left\| f\right\| = \int _0^1 \left|f\right| \]

  1. \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_1\) on \(\mathcal{L}^1(\mathbb {R})\): homogeneity and the triangle inequality hold, but positive definiteness fails;

  2. \(\sqrt{\left|x_1\right|} + \ldots + \sqrt{\left|x_n\right|}\) on \(\mathbb {F}^n\): positive definiteness and the triangle inequality hold, but homogeneity fails;

  3. \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_{1/2}\) on \(\mathbb {F}^n\): homogeneity and positive definiteness hold, but the triangle inequality fails.

SolutionSolution
Enum-item(1)

Both are norms: positive definiteness and homogeneity are obvious from the corresponding properties of \(\left|\cdot \right|\) on \(\mathbb F\); the triangle inequality for \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_1\) and \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_\infty\) follows coordinatewise from \(\left|x_i+y_i\right| \leq \left|x_i\right|+\left|y_i\right|\) (by summing or taking the maximum over \(i\)).

Enum-item(2)

A norm: the same three axioms as in (1), only with an infinite sum instead of a finite one; the series \(\sum \left|x_i+y_i\right|\) converges and is dominated termwise by \(\sum \left|x_i\right|+\sum \left|y_i\right|\).

Enum-item(3)

A norm: \(\left\| f\right\| =0 \iff \sup_X\left|f\right| = 0 \iff f\equiv 0\); homogeneity and the triangle inequality are as for \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_\infty\), except that the \(\sup\) is taken over all of \(X\) rather than over a finite set of indices.

Enum-item(4)

A norm (it is important that we consider exactly \(C[0,1]\), not arbitrary measurable functions): homogeneity and the triangle inequality follow from monotonicity and linearity of the integral: \(\int_0^1\left|f+g\right| \leq \int_0^1(\left|f\right|+\left|g\right|)\). Positive definiteness: if \(\int_0^1\left|f\right| = 0\) for a continuous \(f\geq 0\), then \(f\equiv 0\) (otherwise \(f(x_0)>0\) at some point, and by continuity \(f>c>0\) on an entire interval around \(x_0\), whence \(\int_0^1\left|f\right| \geq c\cdot (\text{length of the interval})>0\) — a contradiction).

As parts (5)–(7) below show (stated directly in the problem): without continuity (5) or when \(p<1\) (7), positive definiteness and the triangle inequality are no longer automatic, and with an exponent \(<1\) in the definition of homogeneity (6), homogeneity itself breaks down.

Every normed space is a metric space: a norm naturally induces a metric. A Banach space is a normed space that is complete with respect to the metric induced by the norm.

ExampleExample 2

Determine whether the following normed spaces are Banach spaces.

  1. \(C[0,1]\) with \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_{\infty }\);

  2. \(\ell^1\) with \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_{1}\);

  3. \(C[0,1]\) with \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_1\);

  4. \(\ell^1\) with \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_{\infty }\).

SolutionSolution
Enum-item(1)

Yes, it is Banach. Let \((f_n)\) be a Cauchy sequence in \((C[0,1],\left\| \cdot \right\|_{\infty })\). For each \(x\in [0,1]\) the numerical sequence \((f_n(x))\) is a Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb F\) (since \(\left|f_n(x)-f_m(x)\right|\leq \left\| f_n-f_m\right\|_{\infty }\)), hence it converges; denote the limit by \(f(x)\). From the Cauchy condition, for every \(\varepsilon >0\) there is \(N\) such that for \(n,m\geq N\) and all \(x\) we have \(\left|f_n(x)-f_m(x)\right|<\varepsilon\); letting \(m\to \infty\), we get \(\left|f_n(x)-f(x)\right|\leq \varepsilon\) for all \(n\geq N\) and all \(x\), i.e., \(f_n\to f\) uniformly. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so \(f\in C[0,1]\), and \(\left\| f_n-f\right\|_{\infty }\to 0\).

Enum-item(2)

Let \((a^{n})_{n\in \mathbb {N}}\) be a Cauchy sequence in \((\ell^1,\left\| \cdot \right\|_{1})\). Then for every \(\varepsilon >0\) there is \(N\) such that for all \(m,n\ge N\)

\[ \sum _{i=1}^{\infty }\left|a^n_i - a^m_i\right| < \varepsilon . \]

In particular, for each fixed \(j\) we have \(\left|a^n_j - a^m_j\right| \le \sum_{i=1}^{\infty }\left|a^n_i - a^m_i\right| < \varepsilon\), so for each \(j\) the sequence \((a^n_j)_{n}\) is a Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb {F}\) and therefore converges; denote \(a_j = \lim_{n\to \infty } a^n_j\). This gives a limiting sequence \(a=(a_1,a_2,\dots )\). It remains to show that \(a\in \ell^1\) and \(a^n\to a\) in the norm \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_{1}\) — this is done by the standard trick of estimating the tail of the sums and passing to the limit inside the sum (using the triangle inequality and the fact that the sums \(\sum_{i=1}^N |a^n_i-a_i|\) are small for large \(n\), while the tail \(\sum_{i>N}|a_i|\) is small for large \(N\)).

Enum-item(3)

No, it is not Banach. Let us exhibit a sequence of continuous functions that is Cauchy in the norm \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_1\) but does not converge (in this norm) to any continuous function. Let

\[ f_n(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \in [0, \tfrac 12-\tfrac 1n], \\ n\left(x - \tfrac 12 + \tfrac 1n\right), & x \in \left(\tfrac 12-\tfrac 1n, \tfrac 12\right), \\ 1, & x \in [\tfrac 12, 1]. \end{cases} \]

Then \(f_n \in C[0,1]\), and with \(g := \; \mathbb {1}_{[1/2,1]}\) (discontinuous at \(\tfrac 12\)) we have \(f_n = g\) outside the interval \(\left(\tfrac 12-\tfrac 1n,\tfrac 12\right)\) of length \(\tfrac 1n\), while on it \(\left|f_n - g\right|\leq 1\), so that

\[ \left\| f_n - g\right\| _1 = \int _0^1 \left|f_n - g\right| \leq \frac1n \xrightarrow [n\to \infty ]{} 0. \]

In particular, \((f_n)\) is Cauchy in \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_1\) (since it converges to \(g\) in this seminorm). If \(f_n \to h\) in \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_1\) for some \(h \in C[0,1]\), then \(h=g\) almost everywhere (the limit in \(L^1\) is unique up to a.e.-equivalence); but no continuous function can agree a.e. with \(g\), which has a jump at \(\tfrac 12\) (a continuous \(h\) agreeing with \(g\) on a dense set on both sides of \(\tfrac 12\) would have to satisfy \(h(\tfrac 12^-)=0\) and \(h(\tfrac 12^+)=1\) — a contradiction with continuity at \(\tfrac 12\)). Hence there is no limit in \(C[0,1]\), and the space \((C[0,1],\left\| \cdot \right\|_1)\) is not complete.

Enum-item(4)

Consider the sequence

\[ a^n = \bigl(1,\tfrac {1}{2},\ldots ,\tfrac {1}{n}, 0, 0, \ldots \bigr), \qquad n \in \mathbb {N}. \]

In the norm \(\left\| \cdot \right\|_{\infty }\) this sequence is Cauchy (the truncations of the sequence become close in the supremum norm), but its limit in \(\ell^1\) with this norm would have to be the sequence \((1,\tfrac 12,\tfrac 13,\ldots )\), whose supremum is \(1\) and which does not belong to \(\ell^1\) in the sense of the sum of absolute values — however, what matters here is that there is no limit lying in \(\ell^1\) (the sequence does not converge in \((\ell^1,\left\| \cdot \right\|_{\infty })\)). Hence the space is not complete.

ExampleExample 3

Let \(X\) be a nonempty set, and let \(b(X)\) be the vector space of bounded functions \(X \to \mathbb {F}\). Prove that if \(b(X)\) is equipped with the norm \(\left\| f\right\| = \sup_X \left|f\right|\), then \(b(X)\) is a Banach space.

SolutionSolution

Let \((f_n)\) be a Cauchy sequence in \((b(X),\left\| \cdot \right\| )\). Then for each \(x\in X\) the sequence of numbers \((f_n(x))\) is Cauchy in \(\mathbb {F}\), hence converges to some \(f(x)\in \mathbb {F}\). This defines a function \(f:X\to \mathbb {F}\). We need to show that \(f\) is bounded and \(f_n \to f\) in the supremum norm. From the Cauchy condition there is \(N\) such that for all \(m,n\ge N\) we have \(\sup_X|f_n - f_m|<\varepsilon\), whence for \(n\ge N\) letting \(m\to\infty\) gives \(\sup_X|f_n - f| \le \varepsilon\). Hence \(f\) is bounded and \(f_n \to f\) in norm, and the space is complete.

2.1.1 Banachness \(\iff\) every absolutely convergent series of vectors converges (in the norm of the space)

An infinite sum of vectors in a normed space:

\[ \sum _{k=1}^{\infty }v_k := \lim _{n \to \infty }\sum _{k=1}^{n}v_k \]

We will call a sequence \(v_1, v_2, \ldots \in V\) a rapid Cauchy sequence if

\[ \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\left\| v_{n+1} - v_{n}\right\| < \infty . \]

Every rapid Cauchy sequence is a Cauchy sequence in the usual sense. Indeed, if

\[ \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\left\| v_{n+1} - v_{n}\right\| < \infty , \]

then for any \(m > n\) we have

\[ \left\| v_m - v_n\right\| \leq \sum _{k=n}^{m-1}\left\| v_{k+1} - v_k\right\| \xrightarrow [n \to \infty ]{} 0, \]

which is exactly the definition of a Cauchy sequence.

Proposition 1 (Equivalent conditions for completeness of a normed space, i.e., for being Banach) Let \(V\) be a normed space. The following conditions are equivalent:

  1. Every Cauchy sequence in \(V\) converges to an element of \(V\) (i.e., \(V\) is a Banach space);

  2. Every rapid Cauchy sequence in \(V\) converges to an element of \(V\);

  3. Absolute convergence of an arbitrary series implies its convergence in norm:

    \[ \sum _{k=1}^{\infty } \left\| v_k\right\| < \infty \; \; \Rightarrow \; \; \sum _{k=1}^{\infty }v_k \text{ converges in } V. \]

2.2 Bounded linear maps

Let \(V\), \(W\) be vector spaces over the same field \(\mathbb {F}\). A map \(T: V \to W\) is called linear if …

The set \(\mathcal{L}(V,W)\) is also a vector space. Now suppose \(V\) and \(W\) are equipped with norms (which may differ, but we will denote them the same way). We will focus on the subset \(\mathcal{B}(V,W)\) of bounded linear maps. Namely, for a linear map \(T: V \to W\) define

\[ \left\| T\right\| := \sup _{f \in V, \left\| f\right\| \leq 1}\left\| Tf\right\| \]

Lemma 1 (Equivalent definitions of the norm of a linear map) Let \(V\) and \(W\) be normed vector spaces, with \(V\neq \{ 0\}\), and let \(T:V\to W\) be a linear operator. Then \[ \begin{align} \left\| T\right\| & = \sup _{f \in V, \left\| f\right\| \leq 1}\left\| Tf\right\| =\sup _{f \in V, \left\| f\right\| < 1}\left\| Tf\right\| = \sup _{f \in V, \left\| f\right\| = 1}\left\| Tf\right\| = \sup _{f \in V\setminus \left\{ 0_V\right\} }\frac{\left\| Tf\right\| }{\left\| f\right\| } = \\ & = \inf \left\{ c\in [0,\infty ): \left\| Tf\right\| \le c\left\| f\right\| \ \forall f\in V\right\} \end{align} \]

A map is called bounded if \(\left\| T\right\| < \infty\).

\[ \mathcal{B}(V,W) = \left\{ T \in \mathcal{L}(V,W) \; : \; \left\| T\right\| < \infty \right\} \]

One can check that \(\mathcal{B}(V,W)\) is also a normed space.

ExampleExample 4

  1. Let \(V = W = C[0,3]\), and let \((Tf)(x) := x^2f(x)\). Verify that \(T\) is linear. Find \(\left\| T\right\|\). Is \(T\) bounded?

  2. Let \(V = W = c_{00}\) with the norm from \(\ell^{\infty }\), and let

    \[ T(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots ) := (a_1, 2a_2, 3a_3, \ldots ) \]

    Verify that \(T\) is linear. Find \(\left\| T\right\|\). Is \(T\) bounded?

SolutionSolution
Enum-item1.

Let \(V = W = C[0,3]\) with the standard supremum norm \(\left\| f\right\| =\sup_{x\in [0,3]}|f(x)|\), and let

\[ Tf(x):=x^2 f(x). \]

Let us check linearity. For any \(f,g\in C[0,3]\) and scalars \(\alpha ,\beta\) we have

\[ T(\alpha f+\beta g)(x)=x^2(\alpha f(x)+\beta g(x))=\alpha x^2 f(x)+\beta x^2 g(x)=\alpha Tf(x)+\beta Tg(x), \]

so the operator \(T\) is linear.

Let us find the norm of the operator. For any \(f\) with \(\left\| f\right\| =1\) we have

\[ \left\| Tf\right\| =\sup _{x\in [0,3]}|x^2 f(x)|\leq \sup _{x\in [0,3]}x^2\cdot \sup _{x\in [0,3]}|f(x)|=9\cdot 1=9. \]

On the other hand, taking a function \(f_0\) such that \(f_0(3)=1\) and \(\left\| f_0\right\| =1\) (for example, the constant function \(f_0\equiv 1\)), we get

\[ \left\| T f_0\right\| =\sup _{x\in [0,3]} x^2 |f_0(x)| \ge 3^2\cdot |f_0(3)|=9. \]

Hence \(\left\| T\right\| =9\). So \(T\) is bounded (and boundedness is expressed by the inequality \(\left\| Tf\right\| \le 9\left\| f\right\|\)).

Enum-item2.

Let \(V = W = c_{00}\), with the norm induced by the \(\ell^\infty\)-norm, and let

\[ T(a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots ):=(1\cdot a_1,\, 2\cdot a_2,\, 3\cdot a_3,\ldots ). \]

Linearity is obvious, since \(T\) acts coordinatewise via the formula \(T(a)_n = n a_n\).

Let us consider boundedness. If \(T\) were bounded, there would exist \(C>0\) such that for all \(a\in c_{00}\)

\[ \left\| T a\right\| _{\infty }=\sup _{n\ge 1} n|a_n| \le C\sup _{n\ge 1}|a_n|=\; C\left\| a\right\| _{\infty }. \]

Take the standard vectors \(e^{(N)}=(0,\dots ,0,1,0,\dots )\), where the \(1\) stands in the \(N\)-th position. Then \(\left\| e^{(N)}\right\|_{\infty }=1\), but

\[ \left\| T e^{(N)}\right\| _{\infty }=\sup _{n} n|e^{(N)}_n| = N. \]

If the inequality held with some constant \(C\), then \(N\le C\) for all \(N\), which is impossible. Hence no finite \(C\) exists, and \(T\) is not bounded (the operator norm would be \(\sup_{n} n = +\infty\)).

Thus, in the first part \(T\) is linear and bounded, \(\left\| T\right\| =9\); in the second part \(T\) is linear but not bounded.

AnswerAnswer
Enum-item1.

Yes. \(\left\| T\right\| = 9\);

Enum-item2.

No.

Proposition 2 (\(W\) Banach \(\; \Rightarrow \;\) \(\mathcal{B}(V, W)\) Banach) Let \(V\) be a normed space, \(W\) a Banach space. Then \(\mathcal{B}(V, W)\) is a Banach space with the norm defined above.

Note that \(V\) itself need not be Banach.

Proposition 3 Let \(V\) and \(W\) be normed vector spaces and \(T:V\to W\) a linear map. The following statements are equivalent:

  1. \(T\) is bounded.

  2. \(T\) is continuous.

  3. \(T\) is continuous at some single point: there exists \(f_0\in V\) such that \(T\) is continuous at \(f_0\).

  4. \(T\) is uniformly continuous (i.e., for every \(\varepsilon >0\) there exists \(\delta >0\) such that \(\left\| Tf - Tg\right\| < \varepsilon\) for all \(f,g\in V\) with \(\left\| f-g\right\| <\delta\)).

  5. The preimage of some open ball around the zero vector is open: there exists \(r > 0\) such that \(T^{-1}\left(B_{r}(0_W)\right)\) is an open subset of \(V\).

If \(W = V\), then a linear map \(T: V\to V\) is called an operator. The set of all operators is denoted \(\mathcal{L}(V)\). The set of all bounded operators on \(V\) is denoted \(\mathcal{B}(V)\).

3 Bases in normed spaces: Hamel basis vs. Schauder basis

Let \(V\) be an arbitrary set. A family \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }\) from \(V\) is a map \(k \mapsto e_k\) from an index set \(\Gamma\) (of arbitrary cardinality) into \(V\). Even though a family is a map, it is denoted like a set (indeed, the image of a family is a subset of \(V\)). Note that, unlike a set, a family may have repeated elements in the sense that different indices may correspond to the same element.

Definition 1 Let \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }\) be a family in a vector space \(V\).

  • The family \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }\) is called linearly independent if every finite subfamily is linearly independent. In other words, if there is no nonempty finite subset \(\Omega\) of \(\Gamma\) and family \(\left\{ \alpha_j\right\}_{j \in \Omega }\) in \(\mathbb {F} \backslash \{ 0\}\) such that \(\sum_{j \in \Omega } \alpha_j e_j=0\).

  • The linear span of the family \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }\) is denoted \(\operatorname {span}\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }\) and is defined as the set of all finite linear combinations of elements of the family. Namely, the set of all sums of the form

    \[ \sum _{j \in \Omega } \alpha _j e_j, \]

    where \(\Omega\) is a finite subset of \(\Gamma\), and \(\left\{ \alpha_j\right\}_{j \in \Omega }\) is a family in \(\mathbb {F}\).

  • A vector space \(V\) is called finite-dimensional if there exists a finite set \(\Gamma\) and a family \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }\) in \(V\) such that \(\operatorname {span}\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }=V\).

  • A vector space is called infinite-dimensional if it is not finite-dimensional.

  • A family in \(V\) is called a Hamel basis of \(V\) if it is linearly independent and its linear span equals \(V\). A Hamel basis is also called an algebraic basis, since it is defined for an arbitrary vector space, normed or not. It makes no use of a norm whatsoever, using only the linear structure of the space itself.

Example: The family \(\{ x^{n}\}_{n\in \{ 0,1,2,\ldots \} }\) is a basis of the space of polynomials.

The main feature of a Hamel basis is that it uses only finite linear combinations. In the finite-dimensional case this is not a problem. In the infinite-dimensional case, however, the space is typically equipped with a norm, and hence it is possible to use infinite sums (series). This gives rise to several problems with the Hamel basis:

  • A Hamel basis does not use the powerful toolkit of series;

  • An infinite-dimensional normed space is typically complete (i.e., Banach). It turns out that even a separable infinite-dimensional Banach space requires an uncountable Hamel basis, and uncountable systems are not very convenient to work with;

  • The proof of existence of Hamel bases for arbitrary infinite-dimensional spaces requires the axiom of choice (moreover, one can show that the statement “every vector space has a Hamel basis” is equivalent to the axiom of choice). Thus, for most Banach spaces there is no constructive method for building a Hamel basis.

In infinite-dimensional normed spaces, more useful than the Hamel basis turns out to be the Schauder basis:

  • A system of vectors \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }\) in a normed space \(V\) is called complete if its linear span is dense in \(V\):

    \[ \overline{\operatorname {span}\left\{ e_k\right\} _{k \in \Gamma }} = V \]

    In other words, any vector in \(V\) can be approximated arbitrarily well by finite linear combinations of vectors from the family. Note that this can not always be done uniquely.

  • If a countable system \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \mathbb {N}}\) is complete in \(V\), and moreover every vector in \(V\) can be approximated by finite linear combinations from \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \mathbb {N}}\) in a unique way, then such a system is called a Schauder basis. Equivalently: a sequence \(\left\{ e_n\right\}\) in a normed space \(V\) is called a Schauder basis of \(V\) if

    \[ \forall v \in V \quad \exists ! \text{ scalars } \alpha _n = \alpha _n(v) \text{ such that } v = \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \alpha _n(v) e_n. \]

  • A basis \(\left\{ e_n\right\}\) is called a normalized Schauder basis if \(\left\| e_n\right\| = 1\) for all \(n\).

In infinite-dimensional normed spaces, the term basis usually refers to a Schauder basis rather than a Hamel basis.

Note that if \(\left\{ e_n\right\}\) is a basis, then the fact that every \(v \in V\) can be written uniquely as \(v = \sum \alpha_n(v) e_n\) implies \(e_n \neq 0\) for all \(n\). As a consequence, every Schauder basis can be normalized: \(\left\{ e_n / \left\| e_n\right\| \right\}_{n \in \mathbb {N}}\) is a normalized Schauder basis for \(V\).

If \(V\) has a Schauder basis \(\left\{ e_n\right\}\), then \(V\) must be separable, since the set of all finite linear combinations \(\sum_{n=1}^N c_n e_n\) with rational \(c_n\) (or with rational real and imaginary parts, if \(c_n\) is complex) forms a countable dense subset of \(V\). The question of whether every separable Banach space has a basis was a long-standing problem known as the Basis Problem. The Swedish mathematician Per Enflo showed in 1972 that there do indeed exist separable Banach spaces with no basis at all, for which he received a live goose from Stanisław Mazur.

4 Linear functionals

4.1 Bounded linear functionals

Linear maps into the scalar field \(\mathbb {F}\) (\(\mathbb {F}=\mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\)) are so important that they receive a special name. A linear functional on a vector space \(V\) is a linear map from \(V\) into \(\mathbb {F}\).

When we regard the scalar field \(\mathbb {F}\) as a normed vector space, the norm \(\| z\|\) of a number \(z \in F\) is understood as the ordinary absolute value \(|z|\). This norm makes \(\mathbb {F}\) a Banach space.

ExampleExample 5

Let \(V = c_{00}\) be the space of sequences \((a_1, a_2, \ldots )\) of elements of \(\mathbb {F}\) such that \(a_k = 0\) for all but finitely many \(k \in \mathbb {Z}^+\). Define \(\varphi : c_{00} \to \mathbb {F}\) by \[ \varphi (a_1, a_2, \ldots ) = \sum _{k=1}^{\infty } a_k. \] Then \(\varphi\) is clearly a linear functional on \(V\). Prove that

  1. if \(c_{00}\) is equipped with the norm \(\| (a_1, a_2, \ldots )\|_1 = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty }|a_k|\), then \(\varphi\) is a bounded linear functional.

  2. if \(c_{00}\) is equipped with the norm \(\| (a_1, a_2, \ldots )\|_{\infty } = \max_{k \in \mathbb {Z}^+}|a_k|\), then \(\varphi\) is not a bounded linear functional.

SolutionSolution
Enum-item1.

We have

\[ \left\| \varphi (a)\right\| = \left|\sum _{k=1}^{\infty }a_k\right| \leq \sum _{k=1}^{\infty }\left|a_k\right| = \left\| a\right\| _{1} \]

Enum-item2.

Consider \(a^{(n)} = (1,1,1,\ldots , 1, 0, 0, \ldots )\) (the first \(n\) places are \(1\), the rest are \(0\)). \(\left\| a^{(n)}\right\|_{\infty } = 1\), while

\[ \left\| \varphi \left(a^{(n)}\right)\right\| = \left|\sum _{k=1}^{\infty } a^{(n)}_k\right| = \left|n\right| \to \infty \]

Let \(V\) and \(W\) be vector spaces and \(T: V \to W\) a linear map. The kernel (null space) of \(T\) is the set

\[ \text{null } T = \{ f \in V : Tf = 0 \} . \]

The kernel of a linear map is a subspace. If \(T\) is a continuous linear map between normed spaces, then \(\text{null } T\) is closed, being the preimage of the closed set \(\{ 0\}\).

The converse is false for general linear maps: a linear map can have closed kernel without being continuous. For linear functionals, however, this is equivalent.

Theorem 1 (Equivalence of boundedness, continuity, and a closed kernel) Let \(V\) be a normed vector space and \(\varphi : V \to \mathbb {F}\) a linear functional not identically zero. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. \(\varphi\) is a bounded linear functional.

  2. \(\varphi\) is a continuous linear functional.

  3. \(\operatorname {null}\varphi\) is a closed subspace of \(V\).

  4. \(\overline{\operatorname {null}\varphi } \neq V\).

4.2 Discontinuous linear functionals

We have already seen discontinuous linear functionals. The main goal of this section is to show that every infinite-dimensional normed vector space has a discontinuous linear functional.

Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be a collection of subsets of a set \(V\). A set \(\Gamma \in \mathcal{A}\) is called a maximal element of \(\mathcal{A}\) if there is no \(\Gamma ' \in \mathcal{A}\) such that \(\Gamma \subsetneq \Gamma '\).

Theorem 2 A subset of a vector space \(V\) is a Hamel basis of \(V\) if and only if it is a maximal element in the collection of all linearly independent subsets of \(V\).

A collection \(C\) of subsets of a set \(V\) is called a chain if \(\Omega , \Gamma \in C\) implies \(\Omega \subseteq \Gamma\) or \(\Gamma \subseteq \Omega\).

Lemma 2 (Zorn’s lemma, simplified version) Let \(V\) be a set and \(\mathcal{A}\) a collection of subsets of \(V\) with the property that the union of all sets in \(C\) belongs to \(\mathcal{A}\) for every chain \(C \subseteq \mathcal{A}\). Then \(\mathcal{A}\) contains a maximal element.

Theorem 3 Every vector space has a Hamel basis.

This theorem only asserts existence of a basis. No one has ever managed to construct an explicit example of a Hamel basis in an infinite-dimensional Banach space.

Theorem 4 Every infinite-dimensional normed vector space has a discontinuous linear functional.

Mn-proofProof

Take a Hamel basis \(\left\{ e_k\right\}_{k \in \Gamma }\) for \(V\). It is infinite, so we may assume \(\mathbb {N} \subset \Gamma\). Define \(\varphi\) as follows: \(\varphi (e_k) := k \left\| e_k\right\|\), where \(k \in \mathbb {N}\). On the remaining basis vectors, let the functional be zero. In other words, if \(f \in V\) is arbitrary, it can be uniquely decomposed as \(f = \sum_{k \in \Gamma '}\alpha_{k}e_k\), where \(\Gamma '\) is a finite subset of \(\Gamma\). Then

\[ \varphi (f) := \sum _{k \in \Gamma ' \cap \mathbb {N}} k\cdot \left\| e_k\right\| \]

\(\varphi\) is linear and discontinuous.

4.3 The Hahn-Banach theorem

In the previous section we proved that every infinite-dimensional normed space has a discontinuous linear functional. But what about continuous ones? The existence of nonzero continuous linear functionals on an arbitrary normed space is nontrivial. The Hahn-Banach theorem guarantees that a bounded linear functional defined on a subspace of a normed space (a finite-dimensional one, say) can be extended to the whole space without increasing its norm.

For example, the quotient space \(\ell^{\infty } / c_{0}\) is an infinite-dimensional Banach space. Yet no one has managed to exhibit an explicit example of a continuous linear functional on it.

In this section we will not need Banachness.

Lemma 3 (Extension lemma (real case)) Let \(V\) be a real normed space, \(U\) a subspace, \(\psi : U \to \mathbb {R}\) a bounded linear functional, and \(h \in V \setminus U\). Then \(\psi\) can be extended to a bounded linear functional \(\varphi : U + \mathbb {R} \to \mathbb {R}\) such that \(\| \varphi \| = \| \psi \|\).

Theorem 5 (Hahn-Banach) Let \(V\) be a normed vector space, \(U\) a subspace, \(\psi : U \to \mathbb {F}\) a bounded linear functional. Then \(\psi\) can be extended to a bounded linear functional \(\varphi\) on all of \(V\) such that \(\| \varphi \| = \| \psi \|\).

Idea of proof for the real case: using Zorn’s lemma we extend the functional step by step, preserving the norm. For the complex case, one passes to the real part of the functional.

4.3.1 Consequences of the Hahn-Banach theorem

The Hahn–Banach theorem points to an important principle in functional analysis: to understand a space, one needs to understand its continuous functionals.

The dual space of a normed space \(V\) is the space \(V' = \mathcal{B}(V, \mathbb {F})\) of all bounded linear functionals on \(V\). This is a Banach space, since the target \(\mathbb {F}\) is Banach (recall that Banachness of \(W\) suffices for Banachness of \(\mathcal{B}(V,W)\)).

ExampleExample 7

Prove that

  1. for any nonzero fixed vector \(f\) of a normed space \(V\) there exists a functional \(\varphi = \varphi_{f} \in V'\) such that \(\| \varphi_{f}\| = 1\) and \(\varphi_{f}(f) = \| f\|\);

  2. the norm of a vector can be found as the maximum over the values of all bounded linear functionals at it:

    \[ \left\| f\right\| = \max _{\substack {\varphi \in V’ \\ \left\| \varphi \right\| = 1}}\left|\varphi (f)\right| \]

SolutionSolution
Enum-item1.

Note that the functional \(\psi (g) := \left\| g\right\|\) does not work, since it is not linear: \(\psi (\alpha f) = \left|\alpha \right| \cdot f \neq \alpha \cdot f\).

Consider the one-dimensional subspace \(U = \operatorname {span}(f) = \left\{ \alpha f \; : \; \alpha \in \mathbb {F}\right\}\). Define on it the functional \(\psi (\alpha f) := \alpha \cdot \left\| f\right\|\). It is linear on \(U\), and

\[ \left\| \psi \right\| = \sup _{\left\| \alpha f\right\| = 1}\left|\psi (\alpha f)\right| = \sup _{\left\| \alpha f\right\| = 1}\left|\alpha \cdot \left\| f\right\| \right| = \sup _{\left\| \alpha f\right\| = 1}\left|\alpha \right| \cdot \left\| f\right\| = \sup _{\left\| \alpha f\right\| = 1}\left\| \alpha f\right\| = 1 \]

Hence \(\psi\) is bounded on \(U\). By the Hahn-Banach theorem, there is a linear functional \(\varphi\) on \(V\) with the same norm, extending \(\psi\), i.e., agreeing with it on \(\operatorname {span}(f)\).

Enum-item2.

Using the previous part, we immediately get one inequality:

\[ \left\| f\right\| = \varphi _f(f) = \left|\varphi _f(f)\right| \leq \max _{\substack {\varphi \in V’ \\ \left\| \varphi \right\| = 1}}\left|\varphi (f)\right| \]

The reverse inequality: if \(\varphi \in V'\), \(\left\| \varphi \right\| = 1\), then by definition \(\left|\varphi (f)\right| \leq \left\| \varphi \right\| \cdot \left\| f\right\| = \left\| f\right\|\). Hence,

\[ \left\| f\right\| \geq \max _{\substack {\varphi \in V’ \\ \left\| \varphi \right\| = 1}}\left|\varphi (f)\right| \]

Let \(U\) be a subspace of a normed space \(V\), \(h \in V\). Then \(f\) belongs to the closure of the subspace \(U\) if and only if every bounded linear functional vanishing on \(U\) also vanishes at \(f\). In other words, \[ f \in \overline{U} \quad \iff \quad \varphi (f) = 0 \text{ for every } \varphi \in V' \text{ such that } \varphi |_{U} \equiv 0 \]

SolutionSolution

  • \(\stackrel{?}{\; \Rightarrow \; }\). Consider an arbitrary \(\varphi \in V'\) such that \(\varphi |_{U} \equiv 0\). Let us prove that \(\varphi (f) = 0\). It is known that \(f \in \overline{U}\). Hence there is a sequence of vectors \(f_1, f_2, \ldots\) in \(U\) converging to \(f\). But \(\varphi (f_n) =0\) for all \(n\) and \(\varphi\) is continuous. Hence \(\varphi (f) = 0\).

  • \(\stackrel{?}{\Leftarrow }\). We need to prove that \(f \in \overline{U}\). Suppose this is not the case. Define \(\psi\) on the subspace \(\operatorname {span}(U, f) = U + \mathbb {F} f\) by

    \[ \psi (g+\alpha f) = \alpha \]

    for \(g \in U\) and \(\alpha \in \mathbb {F}\). Then \(\psi\) is a linear functional on \(U+\mathbb {F} h\), which vanishes on \(U\) and \(\psi (f) = 1\).

    Since \(h \notin \overline{U}\), the closure of the kernel of \(\psi\) is not \(U+\mathbb {F} h\). Thus, by the theorem above, \(\psi\) is a bounded linear functional on \(U+\mathbb {F} h\).

    By the Hahn-Banach theorem, \(\psi\) can be extended to a bounded linear functional \(\varphi\) on \(V\). Thus, we have found \(\varphi \in V^{\prime }\) such that \(\left.\varphi \right|_U=0\), but \(\varphi (f) \neq 0\). Contradiction.

Let \(1 \leq p \leq +\infty\). The conjugate index to \(p\) is the index \(q\) such that

It is easy to show that \(1 \leq q \leq +\infty\).

\[ \left\| \varphi \right\| = \left\| (\alpha , a_{2})\right\| _{q} = \begin{cases} \left|\alpha \right| + \left|a_{2}\right|, & q = 1 \text{ (equivalently: $p = +\infty $),} \\[6pt] \left( \left|\alpha \right|^{q} + \left|a_{2}\right|^{q} \right)^{1/q}, & 1 < q < +\infty \text{ (equivalently: $1 < p < +\infty $),} \\[6pt] \max \left\{ \left|\alpha \right|, \left|a_{2}\right|\right\} , & q = +\infty \text{ (equivalently: $p = 1$)} \end{cases} \]

\[ \| \psi \| = \sup _{\left(x_{1}, 0\right) \in U \backslash \left\{ 0\right\} } \frac{\left|\psi \left(\left(x_{1}, 0\right)\right)\right|}{\left\| \left(x_{1}, 0\right)\right\| _{p}} = \sup _{x_{1} \neq 0} \frac{\left|\alpha x_{1}\right|}{\left|x_{1}\right|} = |\alpha | . \]

5 The Baire category theorem and its consequences

5.1 The Baire category theorem

The Baire category theorem (sometimes called the Baire theorem on categories) is a fundamental result on complete metric spaces. It has important consequences for Banach spaces.

Theorem 6 (Baire) Let \((V, d)\) be a complete metric space.

  1. \(V\) cannot be represented as a countable union of closed sets with empty interior (closed nowhere dense sets).

  2. The intersection of any countable sequence of dense open subsets of \(V\) is nonempty.

The proof of (b) uses a construction of a sequence of nested closed balls whose radii tend to zero. Their centers form a fundamental sequence that converges to a point belonging to all the open sets in the sequence. Statement (a) follows from (b) by passing to complements.

ExampleExample 10

Using the Baire category theorem, prove that

  1. \(\mathbb {R}\) is uncountable;

  2. the irrational numbers cannot be represented as a countable union of sets closed in \(\mathbb {R}\).

SolutionSolution
Enum-item(1)

Suppose \(\mathbb {R}\) is countable. Then it can be represented as

\[ \mathbb {R} = \{ x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots \} \]

Each singleton \(\{ x_n\}\) is closed in \(\mathbb {R}\) and nowhere dense (since a singleton contains no intervals). Then \(\mathbb {R}\) is represented as a countable union of nowhere dense closed sets:

\[ \mathbb {R} = \bigcup _{n=1}^\infty \{ x_n\} \]

But by the Baire category theorem a complete metric space (as \(\mathbb {R}\) with the usual metric) cannot be represented as a countable union of nowhere dense sets. Contradiction.

The resulting contradiction shows that \(\mathbb {R}\) is uncountable.

Enum-item(2)

Suppose the contrary: the irrational numbers can be represented as a countable union of sets closed in \(\mathbb {R}\):

\[ \mathbb {R} \setminus \mathbb {Q} = \bigcup _{n=1}^\infty F_n, \quad \text{where } F_n \text{ are closed in } \mathbb {R} \]

Since \(\mathbb {Q}\) is countable, it can be represented as

\[ \mathbb {Q} = \bigcup _{m=1}^\infty \{ q_m\} \]

where each \(\{ q_m\}\) is closed and nowhere dense. Then all of \(\mathbb {R}\) is represented as:

\[ \mathbb {R} = (\mathbb {R} \setminus \mathbb {Q}) \cup \mathbb {Q} = \left(\bigcup _{n=1}^\infty F_n\right) \cup \left(\bigcup _{m=1}^\infty \{ q_m\} \right) = \bigcup _{k=1}^\infty A_k \]

where \(\{ A_k\}\) is an at most countable family of closed sets. Let us show that each \(A_k\) is nowhere dense. Indeed:

  • \(\{ q_m\}\) is nowhere dense, since singletons contain no intervals.

  • \(F_n\) is also nowhere dense. If some \(F_n\) contained an interval \((a,b)\), then this interval would consist entirely of irrational numbers (since \(F_n \subset \mathbb {R} \setminus \mathbb {Q}\)), which is impossible, since every interval contains rational numbers.

Thus, \(\mathbb {R}\) is represented as a countable union of nowhere dense closed sets, contradicting the Baire category theorem.

Hence the irrational numbers cannot be represented as a countable union of closed sets, i.e., neither can \(\mathbb {R} \setminus \mathbb {Q}\).

5.2 The open mapping theorem (the Banach-Schauder theorem) and the bounded inverse theorem

A map between two topological spaces is called open if the image of every open set (in the domain) is open (in the codomain).

Theorem 7 (Open mapping theorem, Banach-Schauder) Let \(V\) and \(W\) be Banach spaces and \(T: V \to W\) a surjective bounded linear map. Then \(T\) maps open sets to open sets, i.e., for every open \(G \subseteq V\) the set \(T(G)\) is open in \(W\).

5.3 The closed graph theorem

Let \(V\), \(W\) be vector spaces over the same field \(\mathbb {F}\). The Cartesian product \(V \times W\) can readily be given a linear structure: \((v_1, w_1) + (v_2,w_2) := (v_1+v_2, w_1 + w_2)\), etc. It is easy to show that \(V \times W\) is also a vector space over \(\mathbb {F}\).

Theorem 9 Let \(V\), \(W\) be Banach spaces (over the same field \(\mathbb {F}\)). Then the function \[ \left\| (f,g)\right\| := \max \left\{ \left\| f\right\| _{V}, \left\| g\right\| _{W}\right\} \] is a norm on \(V \times W\), and with respect to this norm \(V \times W\) is Banach. Convergence in this norm of \((f_1, g_1), (f_2,g_2), \ldots\) to \((f,g)\) is equivalent to coordinatewise convergence: \(\lim_{n\to \infty }f_n = f\) and \(\lim_{n\to \infty }g_n = g\).

The following result gives a splendid way to show that a linear map between Banach spaces is bounded. The proof is remarkably simple, since most of the work was done in proving the open mapping theorem (which was in turn used to prove the bounded inverse theorem).

Theorem 10 (Closed graph theorem) Let \(V\) and \(W\) be Banach spaces and \(T: V \to W\) a linear map. Then \(T\) is bounded if and only if its graph \(\operatorname {graph}(T) = \{ (f, Tf) : f \in V \}\) is a closed subspace of \(V \times W\): \[ T \in \mathcal{B}(V,W) \quad \iff \quad \operatorname {graph}(T) \text{ is closed in } V \times W \]

Explanation. Let \(T: X \rightarrow Y\) be a linear operator between Banach spaces. Then continuity of \(T\) means that \(T x_i \rightarrow T x\) for every convergent sequence \(x_i \rightarrow x\). On the other hand, closedness of the graph of \(T\) means that for every convergent sequence \(x_i \rightarrow x\) such that \(T x_i \rightarrow y\), we have \(y=T x\). Hence the closed graph theorem states that to verify continuity of \(T\) it suffices to show that \(T x_i \rightarrow T x\) under the additional assumption that \(T x_i\) converges.

In fact, for closedness of the graph of \(T\) it suffices that if \(x_i \rightarrow 0, T x_i \rightarrow y\), then \(y=0\). Indeed, if this condition holds, and if \(\left(x_i, T x_i\right) \rightarrow (x, y)\), then \(x_i-x \rightarrow 0\) and \(T\left(x_i-x\right) \rightarrow y-T x\). Thus \(y=T x\), that is, \((x, y)\) belongs to the graph of \(T\).

5.4 The uniform boundedness principle (the Banach-Steinhaus theorem)

Theorem 11 (Uniform boundedness principle, Banach-Steinhaus theorem) Let \(V\) be a Banach space, \(W\) a normed space, and \(\mathcal{A}\) a family of bounded linear operators from \(V\) into \(W\). If for every \(f \in V\) \[ \sup \{ \| Tf\| : T \in \mathcal{A} \} < \infty \quad \text{(pointwise boundedness)}, \] then \[ \sup \{ \| T\| : T \in \mathcal{A} \} < \infty \quad \text{(uniform boundedness in norm)}. \]

The proof uses the Baire category theorem. The sets \(V_n = \{ f \in V : \| Tf\| \leq n \ \forall T \in \mathcal{A} \}\) are closed. Their union is all of \(V\). By the Baire category theorem, some \(V_n\) has nonempty interior, which allows the operator norms to be estimated uniformly.
