1 Inner Product. Gram Matrix

1.1 Definition of a Euclidean space

In this section the space \(V\) may be either finite-dimensional or infinite-dimensional.

Definition 1 Let \(V\) be a vector space over the field of real or complex numbers \(\mathbb {F}\). An inner product is a function of two variables \(V \times V \to \mathbb {F}\), denoted \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle\), satisfying the following properties:

  1. positive definiteness: \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle \geq 0\) for any \(\overrightarrow {x} \in V\), with \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle = 0\) if and only if \(\overrightarrow {x} = 0_V\).

    The condition \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle \geq 0\) implies, among other things, that \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle\) is necessarily a real number, even if \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\).

  2. Hermitian symmetry (conjugate symmetry): \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle = \overline{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle }\). When \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), this property becomes ordinary symmetry: \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle = \left\langle \overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle\).

  3. linearity in the first argument:

    \[ \left\langle \alpha \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle = \alpha \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle , \qquad \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}_1 + \overrightarrow {x}_2, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle = \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}_1, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle + \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}_2, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle \]

    for any \(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}_1, \overrightarrow {x}_2, \overrightarrow {y} \in V\), \(\alpha \in \mathbb {F}\).

The following terminology is used in the Russian-language literature:

  • A unitary space is a complex (\(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\)) vector space \(V\) together with an inner product defined on it.

  • A Euclidean space is a real (\(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\)) vector space \(V\) together with an inner product defined on it.

In the English-language literature one simply speaks of an inner product space, separately specifying the field over which it is defined whenever this matters for a particular statement. The synonymous term pre-Hilbert space is also used.

Two vectors of an inner product space are called orthogonal if their inner product equals zero: \(\overrightarrow {x} \perp \overrightarrow {y} \stackrel{\text{def}}{\iff } \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle = 0\).

The length or norm of a vector \(\overrightarrow {x} \in V\) from an inner product space is defined as \(\left\| \overrightarrow {x}\right\| := \sqrt{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle }\).

Let us examine the properties of the inner product with respect to the second argument:

\[ \begin{align} \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \beta \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle & = \overline{ \left\langle \beta \overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle } = \overline{\beta \cdot \left\langle \overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle } = \overline{\beta } \cdot \overline{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle } = \overline{\beta } \cdot \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle \\ \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}_1 + \overrightarrow {y_2} \right\rangle & = \overline{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {y}_1 + \overrightarrow {y}_2, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle } = \overline{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {y}_1, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle } + \overline{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {y}_2, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle } = \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}_1 \right\rangle + \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}_2 \right\rangle \end{align} \]

  • If \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\), then the inner product is additive in the second argument but does not have homogeneity in the usual sense: the scalar coefficient from the second argument must be pulled out with complex conjugation. This is why, in general, the inner product is called a sesquilinear form: it is “fully” linear in the first argument, but only “half” linear in the second.

  • If \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), then the inner product becomes linear in the second argument as well, since complex conjugation no longer has any effect. That is, for a real vector space the inner product can be defined as a symmetric, positive-definite bilinear form.

ExampleExample 1

Let \(V = \mathbb {C}^3\). Determine which of the following functions define an inner product:

  1. \(f_1(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) := x_1\overline{y_1} + x_2\overline{y_2} + x_3\overline{y_3} = \overrightarrow {x}^T \cdot \overline{\overrightarrow {y}}\) (\(\overline{\overrightarrow {y}}\) is the column obtained from \(\overrightarrow {y}\) by taking the conjugate of each entry);

  2. \(f_2(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) := x_1\overline{y_1} + x_3\overline{y_3}\);

  3. \(f_3(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) := \left|x_1\overline{y_1}\right| + \left|x_2\overline{y_2}\right| + \left|x_3\overline{y_3}\right|\).

SolutionSolution
Enum-item(1)

  • Positive definiteness: we have

    \[ f_1(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}) = x_1\cdot \overline{x_1} + x_2\cdot \overline{x_2} + x_3\cdot \overline{x_3} = \left|x_1\right|^2 +\left|x_2\right|^2 +\left|x_3\right|^2 \geq 0 \]

    Furthermore, if \(\overrightarrow {x} = (0,0,0)^T\), then clearly \(f_1(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}) = 0\).

    Conversely: suppose that

    \[ f_1(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}) = \left|x_1\right|^2 +\left|x_2\right|^2 +\left|x_3\right|^2 =0 \]

    Since each summand is nonnegative, we get \(\left|x_1\right|^2= \left|x_2\right|^2= \left|x_3\right|^2 = 0\). Hence \(x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = 0\).

  • Conjugate symmetry: using the properties of conjugation of complex numbers, we get

    \[ f_1(\overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x}) = y_1\overline{x_1} + y_2\overline{x_2} + y_3\overline{x_3} = \overline{x_1 \overline{y_1}} + \overline{x_2 \overline{y_2}} + \overline{x_3 \overline{y_3}} = \overline{x_1\overline{y_1} + x_2\overline{y_2} + x_3\overline{y_3}} = \overline{f_1(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y})} \]

  • Linearity in the first argument:

    \[ \begin{align} f_1(\alpha \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) & = \left(\alpha \overrightarrow {x}\right)^T \cdot \overline{\overrightarrow {y}} = \alpha \cdot \overrightarrow {x}^T \cdot \overline{\overrightarrow {y}} = \alpha \cdot f_1(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) \\ f_1(\overrightarrow {x}^1 + \overrightarrow {x}^2 , \overrightarrow {y}) & = \left(\overrightarrow {x}^1 + \overrightarrow {x}^2\right)^T \cdot \overline{\overrightarrow {y}} = \left(\overrightarrow {x}^1\right)^T \cdot \overline{\overrightarrow {y}} + \left(\overrightarrow {x}^2\right)^T \cdot \overline{\overrightarrow {y}} = f_1(\overrightarrow {x}^1, \overrightarrow {y}) + f_1(\overrightarrow {x}^2, \overrightarrow {y}) \end{align} \]

Thus \(f_1\) is indeed an inner product, called the standard inner product.

Note that the proof above readily generalizes to the general case \(V = \mathbb {F}^n\), where \(n \in \mathbb {N}\). That is, on \(\mathbb {F}^n\) the form

\[ (\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) \mapsto x_1\overline{y_1} + \ldots + x_n \overline{y_n} = \overrightarrow {x}^T \cdot \overline{\overrightarrow {y}} \]

defines an inner product.

Enum-item(2)

As in the previous part, it is straightforward to show that \(f_2\) is conjugate symmetric and linear in the first argument. Moreover, \(f_2(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}) \geq 0\). However, \(f_2\) only has nonnegative definiteness, and positive definiteness fails. Indeed, for the nonzero vector \(\overrightarrow {x} = (0,1,0)^T\) we have \(f_2(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}) = 0\).

Enum-item(3)

This function is not linear in the first argument: let

\[ \overrightarrow {x}^1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \overrightarrow {x}^2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \overrightarrow {y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Then

\[ f_3(\overrightarrow {x}^1, \overrightarrow {y}) = \left|1\right| + \left|1\right| + \left|0\right| = 2, \qquad f_3(\overrightarrow {x}^2, \overrightarrow {y}) = \left|-1\right| + \left|1\right| + \left|0\right| = 2 \]

But

\[ f_3(\overrightarrow {x}^1 + \overrightarrow {x}^2, \overrightarrow {y}) = f_3\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right], \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\right) = \left|0\right| + \left|2\right| + \left|0\right| = 2 \neq 4 = f_3(\overrightarrow {x}^1, \overrightarrow {y}) + f_3(\overrightarrow {x}^2, \overrightarrow {y}) \]

ExampleExample 2

Let \(V = \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {R})\) be the space of polynomials with real coefficients. Prove that \[ \left\langle p, q \right\rangle := p(0)q(0) + \int _{-1}^1 p'(t)q'(t) \; dt \] defines an inner product on \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb {R})\).

In this example, of course, \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\). Note that without the term \(p(0)q(0)\) we would not get an inner product — the form \(\int_{-1}^1 p'q'\) vanishes on constants.

SolutionSolution
Enum-item1.

Positive definiteness: we have

\[ \left\langle p, p \right\rangle = p^2(0) + \int _{-1}^{1}\left(p'(t)\right)^2 \; dt \geq 0 \]

Furthermore, if \(p = 0_V\), i.e. \(p(t) = 0\) for all \(t\), then clearly \(\left\langle p, p \right\rangle = 0\).

Conversely: suppose that \(\left\langle p, p \right\rangle = 0\). Suppose that \(p\) is a polynomial of degree \(n\):

\[ p(t) = \alpha _0 + \alpha _1t + \ldots + \alpha _nt^n \]

Hence,

\[ p'(t) = \alpha _1 + 2\alpha _2t + \ldots + n\alpha _nt^{n-1} \]

Since \(p^2(0)\) and the integral of a square are nonnegative numbers, we get \(p^2(0) = \alpha_0 = 0\) and \(\int_{-1}^1 (p'(t))^2 \; dt = 0\). Problem 25.7 of the homework asks you to prove that \(\int_{-1}^1 fg\) is an inner product on the space of functions continuous on \([-1,1]\). Let us use this result. In particular, let us use positive definiteness: if \(\int_{-1}^1f^2(t)dt = 0\), then \(f\) is identically zero on \([-1,1]\). In our case \(\int_{-1}^1 (p'(t))^2 dt = 0\), hence \(p'(t)\) is identically zero. Thus \(p'\) is a polynomial with uncountably many roots. This means that all of its coefficients are zero:

\[ \alpha _1 = 0, \quad 2\alpha _2 = 0, \quad \ldots , \quad n\alpha _n = 0 \]

That is, \(\alpha_1 = \ldots = \alpha_n = 0\). Above we showed that \(\alpha_0 = 0\). So we have proved that \(p\) is the zero polynomial.

Enum-item2.

Symmetry:

\[ \left\langle p, q \right\rangle := p(0)q(0) + \int _{-1}^1 p'(t)q'(t) \; dt = q(0)p(0) + \int _{-1}^1 q'(t)p'(t)dt = \left\langle q, p \right\rangle \]

Enum-item3.

Linearity in the first argument (using linearity of differentiation and of the integral):

\[ \begin{align} \left\langle \alpha p, q \right\rangle & = (\alpha p)(0) \cdot q(0) + \int _{-1}^1 (\alpha p)'(t) \cdot q'(t) \; dt = \alpha p(0)q(0) + \alpha \int _{-1}^1 p'(t)q'(t)dt = \alpha \cdot \left\langle p, q \right\rangle \\ \left\langle p_1 + p_2, q \right\rangle & = (p_1 + p_2)(0) \cdot q(0) + \int _{-1}^1 (p_1 + p_2)'(t)\cdot q'(t) \; dt = p_1(0)q(0) + p_2(0)q(0) + \int _{-1}^1 p'_1(t) q'(t) + \int _{-1}^1 p'_2(t) q'(t) = \\ & = \left\langle p_1, q \right\rangle + \left\langle p_2, q \right\rangle \end{align} \]

1.2 Inner product in coordinates

In this section \(V\) is a finite-dimensional inner product space.

Let \(V\), with inner product \(\left\langle \cdot , \cdot \right\rangle\), be equipped with a basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\). The Gram matrix \(\Gamma\) for the inner product \(\left\langle \cdot , \cdot \right\rangle\) is the matrix

\[ \Gamma = \begin{pmatrix} \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_1 \right\rangle & \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_2 \right\rangle & \cdots & \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_n \right\rangle \\ \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_2, \overrightarrow {e}_1 \right\rangle & \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_2, \overrightarrow {e}_2 \right\rangle & \cdots & \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_2, \overrightarrow {e}_n \right\rangle \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_n, \overrightarrow {e}_1 \right\rangle & \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_n, \overrightarrow {e}_2 \right\rangle & \cdots & \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_n, \overrightarrow {e}_n \right\rangle \\ \end{pmatrix} \]

ExampleExample 3

Let \(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \in V\) be two vectors, \([\overrightarrow {x}], [\overrightarrow {y}] \in \mathbf{F}^n\) their coordinate columns in the basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\), and \(\Gamma\) the Gram matrix of the inner product in this basis. How can \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle\) be expressed in terms of the coordinates of the vectors and the Gram matrix?

SolutionSolution

We have

\[ \begin{align} \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle & = \left\langle \sum _{i=1}^n [\overrightarrow {x}]_i \overrightarrow {e}_i, \sum _{j=1}^n [\overrightarrow {y}]_j \overrightarrow {e}_j \right\rangle = \sum _{i=1}^n \sum _{j=1}^n \left\langle [\overrightarrow {x}]_i \overrightarrow {e}_i, [\overrightarrow {y}]_j \overrightarrow {e}_j \right\rangle = \sum _{i=1}^n \sum _{j=1}^n [\overrightarrow {x}]_i \cdot \overline{[\overrightarrow {y}]_j} \cdot \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_i, \overrightarrow {e}_j \right\rangle = \\ & = [\overrightarrow {x}]^T \cdot \Gamma \cdot \overline{[\overrightarrow {y}]} \end{align} \]

where \(\overline{[\overrightarrow {y}]}\) is the column obtained from the column \([\overrightarrow {y}]\) by taking the conjugate of each entry.

In particular, if \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), then

\[ \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle = [\overrightarrow {x}]^T \cdot \Gamma \cdot [\overrightarrow {y}] \]

2 Geometry of an Inner Product Space

In this section the space \(V\) is a finite-dimensional inner product space.

2.1 Orthogonal complement of a subspace

If \(U \subset V\) is some subspace of \(V\) (or, more generally, simply an arbitrary set of vectors), then its orthogonal complement is defined as

\[ U^{\perp } := \left\{ \overrightarrow {v} \in V \; : \; \left\langle \overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {u} \right\rangle = 0, \, \forall \overrightarrow {u} \in U\right\} \]

It is easy to show that \(U^\perp\) is a subspace, even if \(U\) itself is not a subspace. Moreover, if \(U\) is a subspace, then

\[ V = U \oplus U^\perp . \]

From this, in particular, it follows that \(\left(U^\perp \right)^\perp = U\).

A basis is called orthogonal if all of its vectors are pairwise orthogonal. Equivalently, a basis is orthogonal if its Gram matrix is diagonal. Furthermore, a basis is called orthonormal if it is orthogonal and the length of each vector equals \(1\). Equivalently, a basis is called orthonormal if its Gram matrix is the identity matrix.

ExampleExample 4

A subspace \(U\) is given as the span of vectors whose coordinate columns in an orthonormal basis are \[ \overrightarrow {a}_1 = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 \\ -5 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right), \qquad \overrightarrow {a}_2 = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right) \] Find

  1. a matrix for the HSLE defining \(U^{\perp }\);

  2. a basis of \(U^{\perp }\).

SolutionSolution

We identify vectors with their coordinate columns (elements of \(\mathbb {R}^{3}\)) in this orthonormal basis. Note that since the basis is orthonormal, the Gram matrix is the identity, and

\[ \left\langle \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \right\rangle = \overrightarrow {x}^T \cdot \overrightarrow {y} \]

Note that if \(U\) is a subspace with basis \((\overrightarrow {a}_i)_1^k\), then the subspace orthogonal to it consists of exactly those vectors that are orthogonal to the basis vectors. That is, there is no need to check orthogonality against every other vector of \(U\); it suffices to check it against the basis vectors only.

Enum-item(1)

We have

\[ \begin{align} U^\perp & = \left\{ \overrightarrow {b} \in V \; : \; \left\langle \overrightarrow {b}, \overrightarrow {a}_1 \right\rangle = \left\langle \overrightarrow {b}, \overrightarrow {a}_2 \right\rangle = 0\right\} = \\ & = \left\{ \overrightarrow {b} \in V \; : \; \overrightarrow {a}_1^T \overrightarrow {b} = \overrightarrow {a}_2^T \overrightarrow {b} = 0\right\} = \\ & = \left\{ \overrightarrow {b} \in V \; : \; A^T \cdot \overrightarrow {b} = \overrightarrow {0}\right\} ,\\ \end{align} \]

where \(A\) is the matrix whose columns are the given \(\overrightarrow {a}_1\), \(\overrightarrow {a}_2\):

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -5 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -5 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

Enum-item(2)

A basis of \(U^\perp\) is an FSS of the system \(A^T \overrightarrow {b} = \overrightarrow {0}\). Let us find the FSS. We have

\[ A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -5 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -5 & 1 \\ 0 & -4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -5 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix}\sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -3/2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix} \]

A basis of \(U^\perp\):

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 3/2 \\ 1/2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

AnswerAnswer
Enum-item(1)

\(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -5 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\);

Enum-item(2)

\(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 3/2 \\ 1/2 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\).

ExampleExample 5

A subspace \(U\) is given in an orthonormal basis by the HSLE \(A^T\xi = \overrightarrow {0}\), where \[ A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -5 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \] Find

  1. a basis of the subspace \(U^\perp\);

  2. the HSLE defining \(U^\perp\).

SolutionSolution

This is the inverse problem to the previous one.

Enum-item(1)

\(U\) is the solution set of the system \(A^T \xi = \overrightarrow {0}\), i.e. the set of vectors orthogonal (in an orthonormal basis the inner product is the standard one) to the rows of the matrix \(A^T\). Enum-item(2)

The HSLE for \(U^\perp\) is built from a basis of \(\left(U^\perp \right)^\perp = U\): from the previous example a basis of \(U\) is the vector \((3/2, 1/2, 1)^T\), and the orthogonality condition against it gives (after multiplying by \(2\)) the equation

\[ 3y_1 + y_2 + 2y_3 = 0 \]

AnswerAnswer
Enum-item(1)

2 vectors: \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 \\ -5 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right), \left(\begin{smallmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\);

Enum-item(2)

\(3y_1 + y_2 + 2y_3 = 0\).

Matrices and vectors: \(\mathbf{A}_{424} = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\).

SolutionSolution

We proceed as in the previous example, now with two-dimensional \(\mathcal{L}\) and \(\mathcal{L}^\perp\). A basis of \(\mathcal{L}\) is an FSS of the system \(A\xi = \overrightarrow {0}\): \[ \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \; \Rightarrow \; \overrightarrow {l}_1 = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right], \; \overrightarrow {l}_2 = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right] \]

Enum-item(1)

The condition \(\overrightarrow {x} \in \mathcal{L}^\perp\): \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {l}_i, \overrightarrow {x} \right\rangle = \overrightarrow {l}_i^T \Gamma \overrightarrow {x} = 0\), \(i = 1, 2\). Let us compute the rows of the system:

\[ \overrightarrow {l}_1^T \Gamma = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \overrightarrow {l}_2^T \Gamma = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

2.2 Orthogonal projections

Let \(U \subset V\) be a subspace of some Euclidean space. It is known that \(V\) then decomposes as a direct sum of \(U\) and its orthogonal complement:

\[ V = U \oplus U^\perp \]

That is, any vector \(\overrightarrow {v}\) has a unique decomposition \(\overrightarrow {v} = \overrightarrow {u} + \overrightarrow {w}\), where \(\overrightarrow {u} \in U\), \(\overrightarrow {w} \in U^\perp\). \(\overrightarrow {u} =: \operatorname {pr}_U(\overrightarrow {v})\) is called the orthogonal projection of the vector \(\overrightarrow {v}\) onto \(U\). The vector \(\overrightarrow {w} =: \operatorname {ort}_U(\overrightarrow {v})\) is called the orthogonal component of the vector \(\overrightarrow {v}\) with respect to the subspace \(U\).

Why are orthogonal bases in subspaces useful? They make it very easy to find projections onto the subspaces they span.

Theorem 1 Suppose an orthonormal basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^k\) is chosen in a subspace \(U \subset V\) of a Euclidean space. Then the value of the orthogonal projection of \(\overrightarrow {v}\) onto \(U\) can be found as follows:

\[ \operatorname {pr}_U(\overrightarrow {v}) = \left\langle \overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {e}_1 \right\rangle \cdot \overrightarrow {e}_1 + \left\langle \overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {e}_2 \right\rangle \cdot \overrightarrow {e}_2 + \cdots + \left\langle \overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {e}_k \right\rangle \cdot \overrightarrow {e}_k \]

If the basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^k\) is only orthogonal, but not normalized, then the formula is as follows:

\[ \operatorname {pr}_U(\overrightarrow {v}) = \frac{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {e}_1 \right\rangle }{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_1 \right\rangle } \cdot \overrightarrow {e}_1 + \frac{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {e}_2 \right\rangle }{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_2, \overrightarrow {e}_2 \right\rangle }\cdot \overrightarrow {e}_2 + \cdots + \frac{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {e}_k \right\rangle }{ \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_k, \overrightarrow {e}_k \right\rangle } \cdot \overrightarrow {e}_k \]

2.3 Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization

Let an arbitrary basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\) be chosen in a Euclidean space \(V\). We want to build from these vectors new bases \((\overrightarrow {e}'_i)_1^n\) and \((\overrightarrow {e}''_i)_1^n\) such that

  1. \((\overrightarrow {e}'_i)_1^n\) is orthogonal, and \((\overrightarrow {e}''_i)_1^n\) is orthonormal;

  2. \(U_k := \operatorname {span}\left(\overrightarrow {e}_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}_k\right) = \operatorname {span}\left(\overrightarrow {e}'_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}'_k\right) = \operatorname {span}\left(\overrightarrow {e}''_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}''_k\right)\), \(\forall k \in \left\{ 1, \ldots , n\right\}\);

  3. The change-of-basis matrix to the primed basis is upper triangular with ones on the main diagonal.

The basis \((\overrightarrow {e}'_i)_1^n\) is called the orthogonalization of the basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\), and \((\overrightarrow {e}''_i)_1^n\) is called its orthonormalization. It can be found using the Gram-Schmidt algorithm. Algorithm:

  • \(\overrightarrow {e}_1' := \overrightarrow {e}_1\), \(\overrightarrow {e}''_1 = \frac{1}{\left\| \overrightarrow {e}'_1\right\| } \overrightarrow {e}'_1\);

  • \(\overrightarrow {e}_{i+1}' := \overrightarrow {e}_{i+1} - \operatorname {pr}_{U_{i}}(\overrightarrow {e}_{i+1})\), \(i =1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots , n-1\). Here

    \[ U_{i} = \operatorname {span}\left(\overrightarrow {e}'_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}'_{i}\right) = \operatorname {span}\left(\overrightarrow {e}''_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}''_{i}\right) \]

    and \(\overrightarrow {e}''_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}''_{i}\) is an orthonormal basis of \(U_{i}\). Hence

    \[ \overrightarrow {e}_{i+1}' := \overrightarrow {e}_{i+1} - \operatorname {pr}_{U_{i}}(\overrightarrow {e}_{i+1}) = \overrightarrow {e}_{i+1} - \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_{i+1}, \overrightarrow {e}''_{1} \right\rangle \overrightarrow {e}''_{1} - \ldots - \left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_{i+1}, \overrightarrow {e}''_{i} \right\rangle \overrightarrow {e}''_{i} \]

    Next, \(\overrightarrow {e}''_{i+1} = \frac{1}{\left\| \overrightarrow {e}'_{i+1}\right\| } \overrightarrow {e}'_{i+1}\).

A square matrix \(C \in \mathbb {R}^{n\times n}\) is called orthogonal if \(C^T C = E\). Equivalently, a square matrix is called orthogonal if it is invertible and \(C^{-1} = C^T\).

Note that for an arbitrary matrix \(C \in \mathbb {R}^{m \times n}\), the \((i,j)\) entry of \(C^TC\) equals the sum of products of the corresponding entries of the \(i\)-th row of \(C^T\) (i.e. the \(i\)-th column of \(C\)) and the \(j\)-th column of \(C\). That is, essentially the \((i,j)\) entry of \(C^TC\) is the value of the standard inner product (in \(\mathbb {R}^{m}\)) of columns \(i\) and \(j\) of \(C\). In particular, if \(m=n\) and the columns of \(C\) form a basis of \(\mathbb {R}^{n}\) (an equivalent condition: \(C\) is invertible), then \(C^TC\) is the Gram matrix for this basis. As we recall, if the Gram matrix of a basis equals the identity matrix, then this basis is orthonormal. Thus we obtain yet another equivalent condition for a matrix to be orthogonal: its columns form an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb {R}^{n}\) with the standard inner product1.

Another useful fact: \(C\) is orthogonal if and only if \(C^T\) is orthogonal.

3 Linear Maps on an Inner Product Space. Self-Adjoint and Orthogonal Operators

In this section \(V\) is a finite-dimensional inner product space.

Let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) be a linear operator. A linear operator \(T^*: V \rightarrow V\) is called the adjoint of \(T\) if

\[ \left\langle T\overrightarrow {u}, \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle = \left\langle \overrightarrow {u}, T^*(\overrightarrow {v}) \right\rangle , \qquad \forall \overrightarrow {u}, \overrightarrow {v} \in V \]

Theorem 3 For any linear operator \(T\) on \(V\) (finite- or even infinite-dimensional), there exists a unique adjoint linear operator \(T^*\).

Mn-proofProof

We prove the theorem for the finite-dimensional case. Let \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\) be an arbitrary fixed orthonormal basis of \(V\) (one can always be found by taking an arbitrary basis and then orthogonalizing and normalizing it). Let \(A_T\) be the matrix of the operator \(T\) in this basis, and \([\overrightarrow {v}]\), \([\overrightarrow {u}]\) the coordinate columns of the vectors \(\overrightarrow {v}\), \(\overrightarrow {u}\) in this basis. Then \([T\overrightarrow {u}] = A_T \cdot [\overrightarrow {u}]\) and \[ \left\langle T\overrightarrow {u}, \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle = [T\overrightarrow {u}]^T \cdot \overline{[\overrightarrow {v}]} = [\overrightarrow {u}]^T \cdot A_T^T \cdot \overline{[\overrightarrow {v}]} = [\overrightarrow {u}]^T \cdot \overline{\overline{A_T^T} \cdot [\overrightarrow {v}]} = \left\langle \overrightarrow {u}, T^* \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle \] That is, the operator \(T^*\) is uniquely defined in the chosen basis by the matrix \(\overline{A_T^T}\): \[ A_{T^*} = \overline{A_T^T} \]

The matrix \(\overline{A^T}\), obtained from the original matrix \(A\) by transposing and taking the complex conjugate, is called the Hermitian adjoint (or conjugate transpose) of the original matrix. Notation: \(A^* = \overline{A^T}\). Thus, in an ONB, the matrix of the adjoint operator can be obtained from the matrix of the original operator by taking the Hermitian adjoint: \(A_{T^*} = A_T^*\).

Note that when \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), the Hermitian adjoint reduces simply to the transpose. That is, in a Euclidean space (a real inner product space), the matrix of the adjoint operator in an ONB is the transpose of the matrix of the original operator.

Adjunction in the space of operators is in many ways analogous to conjugation of complex numbers. For example, the following properties hold.

Theorem 4 (Properties of the operator adjoint) Let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) be an operator on \(V\). Then

  1. \((S+T)^* = S^* + T^*\) for any \(S \in \mathcal{L}(V)\);

  2. \((\lambda T)^* = \overline{\lambda } T^*\) for any \(\lambda \in \mathbb {F}\);

  3. \(\left(T^*\right)^* = T\);

  4. \((ST)^* = T^*S^*\) for any \(S \in \mathcal{L}(V)\);

  5. \(I^* = I\), where \(I\) is the identity operator;

  6. If \(T\) is invertible, then so is \(T^*\), and \(\left(T^*\right)^{-1} = \left(T^{-1}\right)^{*}\).

Further on we will see even more analogies with conjugation in \(\mathbb {C}\).

3.1 Self-adjoint and skew-adjoint operators

If \(T^* =T\), then the operator \(T\) is called self-adjoint. If \(T^* = -T\), then the operator is called skew-adjoint. Equivalently, an operator \(T\) is self-adjoint if

\[ \left\langle T\overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {u} \right\rangle = \left\langle \overrightarrow {v}, T\overrightarrow {u} \right\rangle , \quad \forall \overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {u} \in V \]

  • When \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), a self-adjoint operator is also called symmetric, and a skew-adjoint operator is called skew-symmetric or antisymmetric. This terminology comes from the fact that in an ONB the matrix of \(T\), if it is self-adjoint, must be symmetric:

    \[ A_T = A_{T^*} = A_T^T \]

    And if it is skew-adjoint, then its matrix in an ONB is skew-symmetric.

    The converse statements also hold: if in an ONB the matrix of an operator is symmetric (skew-symmetric, resp.), then it is self-adjoint (skew-adjoint, resp.).

  • When \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\), a self-adjoint operator is also called Hermitian, and a skew-adjoint operator is called skew-Hermitian or anti-Hermitian. The matrix of a Hermitian operator (skew-Hermitian, resp.) in an ONB must satisfy \(A_T = A_T^*\) (\(A_{T} = -A_T^*\), resp.). This is the source of the terminology for matrices as well: if a complex matrix equals its own Hermitian adjoint (\(A = A^*\)), it is called Hermitian. If \(A = -A^*\), it is called skew-Hermitian.

    The converse statements also hold: if in an ONB the matrix of an operator is Hermitian (skew-Hermitian, resp.), then it is self-adjoint (skew-adjoint, resp.).

A self-adjoint operator behaves much like a complex number that equals its own conjugate, i.e. like a real number viewed as an element of \(\mathbb {C}\). Skew-symmetric operators, correspondingly, behave like \(\left\{ z \in \mathbb {C} \; : \; \operatorname {Re}z = 0\right\}\), i.e. like complex numbers with no real part.

A complex number \(z\) can be represented as the sum of a real and an imaginary part: \(z = a + ib = a + c\) (\(c = ib\)), where \(a = \overline{a}\), and \(c = -\overline{c}\). Such a representation is called the algebraic form of a complex number. Let us state an analogous theorem for operators. Note that it holds both when \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\) and when \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\).

Theorem 5 (Algebraic form of an operator) Let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\). Then the operator can be represented uniquely in the form \(T = A + C\), where \(A\) is self-adjoint and \(C\) is skew-adjoint, with \(A = \frac{T + T^*}{2}\) and \(C = \frac{T - T^*}{2}\).

Mn-proofProof

It is straightforward to check that \(\frac{T + T^*}{2}\) is a self-adjoint operator, and \(\frac{T - T^*}{2}\) is skew-adjoint. This proves existence of the decomposition.

Uniqueness: suppose \(T = A + C\), where \(A\) is self-adjoint and \(C\) is skew-adjoint. Then \(T^* = A^* + C^* = A - C\), i.e. \(2A = T + T^*\). The operator \(A\) is thus uniquely determined as \(\frac{T + T^*}{2}\). Similarly for \(C\).

The following theorem holds only when \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\), i.e. when \(V\) is a unitary space.

Theorem 6 (Algebraic form of an operator in unitary spaces) Let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\), \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\). Then the operator can be represented uniquely in the form \(T = A + iB\), where \(A\), \(B\) are self-adjoint operators, with \(A = \frac{T + T^*}{2}\) and \(B = \frac{T - T^*}{2i}\).

3.2 Normal operators

An operator \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) is called normal if it commutes with its adjoint: \(TT^* = T^*T\). Check for yourself that self-adjoint operators, skew-adjoint operators, and also unitary operators (considered below) are all normal. Hence the properties given in the following theorem apply to all of these operators.

Theorem 7 (Properties of a normal operator) Let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) be a normal operator. Then

  1. \(\operatorname {Ker} T=\operatorname {Ker} T^*\);

  2. \(\operatorname {Im}T=\operatorname {Im} T^*\);

  3. \(V=\operatorname {Ker} T \oplus \operatorname {Im} T\);

  4. \(T-\lambda I\) is normal for any \(\lambda \in \mathbb {F}\);

  5. If \(\overrightarrow {v} \in V\) and \(\lambda \in \mathbb {F}\), then \(T\overrightarrow {v}=\lambda \overrightarrow {v}\) if and only if \(T^* \overrightarrow {v}=\overline{\lambda } \overrightarrow {v}\);

  6. Eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to different eigenvalues are orthogonal.

3.3 Spectral theorems

Recall that a diagonal matrix is a square matrix that is everywhere zero except possibly on the diagonal. Recall that an operator on \(V\) is called diagonalizable if it has a diagonal matrix with respect to some basis of \(V\). Recall also that this happens if and only if there exists a basis of \(V\) consisting of eigenvectors of the operator.

The nicest operators on \(V\) are those for which there exists an orthonormal basis of \(V\) with respect to which the operator has a diagonal matrix. These are exactly the operators \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) for which there exists an orthonormal basis of \(V\) consisting of eigenvectors of \(T\). Our goal in this section is to prove the spectral theorem, which characterizes these operators. It turns out that if \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), then exactly the self-adjoint operators are diagonalizable in an ONB. If \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\), then a wider class of operators is diagonalizable in an ONB, namely the normal operators.

The spectral theorem is probably the most useful tool in the study of operators on inner product spaces.

Theorem 8 (Spectral theorem for the case \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\)) Let \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), and let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) be a linear operator. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. \(T\) is self-adjoint;

  2. \(T\) has a diagonal matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis of \(V\);

  3. \(V\) has an ONB consisting of eigenvectors of \(T\);

3.4 Unitary operators

Let \(V\) be an inner product space, \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) an operator. \(T\) is called unitary if \(T^*T = TT^* = I\), i.e. \(T\) is invertible and its inverse coincides with its adjoint.

Note that if \(z \in \mathbb {C}\), then the condition \(z\overline{z} = \overline{z}z = 1\) is equivalent to the condition \(\left|z\right| = 1\), i.e. \(z\) lies on the complex unit circle. Below we will see that unitary operators (among all operators) behave similarly to the unit circle (as a subset of \(\mathbb {C}\)).

Many authors use the term “unitary” for an operator only when \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\). When \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), an operator with the property \(TT^*= T^*T = I\) is called orthogonal. In these notes we will generally use the term unitary both for real and for complex spaces, since most statements about such operators do not depend on the field (\(\mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\)), and the term unitary is more universal.

  • If \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\), then in an ONB \(A_{T^*} = A_T^T\), and the matrix of a unitary (i.e. orthogonal) operator \(T\) must satisfy

    \[ A_TA_T^T = A_T^T A_T = E \]

    A square matrix \(A\) satisfying \(AA^T = A^TA = E\) is called orthogonal. We already discussed above that its columns form an ONB of \(\mathbb {R}^{n}\) with the standard inner product.

    An operator is orthogonal \(\iff\) its matrix is orthogonal in an arbitrary ONB.

  • If \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\), then in an ONB \(A_{T^*} = A_T^{*}\), and the matrix of a unitary operator \(T\) must satisfy

    \[ A_TA_T^* = A_T^* A_T = E \]

    A square matrix \(A\) satisfying \(AA^* = A^*A = E\) is called unitary. Its columns form an ONB of \(\mathbb {C}^{n}\) with the standard inner product.

    An operator is unitary \(\iff\) its matrix is unitary in an arbitrary ONB.

Let us state equivalent definitions of a unitary operator.

Theorem 10 Let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\). Let \((\overrightarrow {e}_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}_n)\) be an arbitrary ONB of \(V\), and \(A_T\) the matrix of \(T\) in this ONB. The following conditions are equivalent:

  1. \(T\) is a unitary operator;

  2. \(T\) is an isometric operator, i.e. an operator that preserves the norm: \(\left\| T\overrightarrow {v}\right\| = \left\| \overrightarrow {v}\right\|\) for any \(\overrightarrow {v} \in V\);

  3. \(T\) preserves the inner product, i.e. \(\left\langle T\overrightarrow {u}, T\overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle = \left\langle \overrightarrow {u}, \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle\) for any \(\overrightarrow {u}\), \(\overrightarrow {v}\);

  4. \(T\) maps an arbitrarily chosen ONB to another ONB: \((T\overrightarrow {e}_1, \ldots , T\overrightarrow {e}_n)\) is an ONB.

  5. The columns of the matrix \(A_T\) form an ONB of \(\mathbb {F}^n\) with the standard inner product;

  6. \(T^*\) is a unitary operator.

The following theorems demonstrate the analogy between unitary operators and the complex unit circle.

Theorem 11 Let \(T\) be a unitary operator, \(\lambda\) one of its eigenvalues. Then \(\left|\lambda \right| = 1\).

In particular, this theorem states that when \(\mathbb {F}=\mathbb {R}\), any eigenvalue of a unitary (i.e. orthogonal) operator can only be \(1\) or \(-1\).

Here is another equivalent condition for unitarity in the case \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\).

Theorem 12 Let \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\) and \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\). Then the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. \(S\) is a unitary operator;

  2. \(V\) has an ONB consisting of eigenvectors of \(T\), and all eigenvalues of \(T\) lie on the complex unit circle.

  • Step 1: \(T\) has an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\pm 1\).     (an orthogonal operator preserves the inner product). That is, \(T\overrightarrow {u} \in U\).

ExampleExample 20

Prove that the orthogonal operators of a Euclidean space \(V\) form a group under the operation of composition.

SolutionSolution

First we need to check whether the orthogonal operators are closed under composition. That is, we need to check whether composition really is an operation on the set of orthogonal operators. Let \(S\), \(T\) be arbitrary orthogonal operators. Then, writing \(S \circ T = ST\) for brevity,

\[ (ST)^{-1} = T^{-1}S^{-1} = T^*S^* = (ST)^* \]

so \(ST\) is indeed orthogonal.

Group properties:

  • Associativity: \(S \circ (T \circ R) \stackrel{?}{=} (S \circ T) \circ R\). We have

    \[ \bigg(S \circ (T \circ R)\bigg)(\overrightarrow {v}) = S\bigg((T \circ R)(\overrightarrow {v})\bigg) = S\bigg(T\big(R(\overrightarrow {v})\big)\bigg) = \bigg((S \circ T) \circ R\bigg)(\overrightarrow {v}), \quad \forall \overrightarrow {v} \in V \]

    Associativity holds.

  • Existence of an identity element: \(I^* = I\), \(I^*I= II^* = I\), i.e. \(I\) is orthogonal.

  • Existence of an inverse element: an orthogonal \(T\) is nondegenerate (\(T^{-1} = T^*\)), and its inverse is also orthogonal:

    \[ \left(T^{-1}\right)^* = \left(T^*\right)^{-1} = \left(T^{-1}\right)^{-1} \quad \; \Rightarrow \; \quad \left(T^{-1}\right)^* \cdot T^{-1} = I \]

3.5 Positive and negative self-adjoint operators

An operator \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) is called nonnegative if it is self-adjoint and

\[ \left\langle T\overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle \geq 0 \quad \forall \overrightarrow {v} \in V \]

A self-adjoint operator is called positive if

\[ \left\langle T\overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle > 0 \quad \forall \overrightarrow {v} \in V\setminus \left\{ 0_V\right\} \]

The properties of positive operators in the theorem below correspond to properties of nonnegative numbers in \(\mathbb {C}\). In particular, a number \(z \in \mathbb {C}\) is nonnegative if and only if it has a nonnegative (i.e. lying in \(\mathbb {R}_+\) as a subset of \(\mathbb {C}\)) square root, which corresponds to condition (d). Also, \(z\) is nonnegative if and only if it has a real square root, which corresponds to condition (e). Finally, \(z\) is nonnegative if and only if there exists \(w \in \mathrm{C}\) such that \(z=\bar{w} w\), which corresponds to condition (f).

An operator \(R \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) is called a square root of the operator \(T\) if \(R^2 = T\).

Theorem 13 (Properties of nonnegative and positive operators) Let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\). The following conditions are equivalent:

  1. \(T\) is a nonnegative (positive, resp.) operator.

  2. \(T\) is self-adjoint and all eigenvalues of \(T\) are nonnegative (positive, resp.).

  3. In some ONB the matrix of \(T\) is a diagonal matrix with nonnegative (positive, resp.) numbers on the diagonal.

  4. \(T\) has a nonnegative (positive, resp.) square root.

  5. \(T\) has a self-adjoint (self-adjoint nondegenerate, resp.) square root.

  6. \(T = R^*R\) for some operator \(R \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) (for some nondegenerate operator \(R \in \mathcal{L}(V)\), resp.).

ExampleExample 21

For an arbitrary linear operator \(T\) on an inner product space, the operator \(T^* T\) is nonnegative, and it is positive if and only if \(T\) is nondegenerate. Prove this.

SolutionSolution

The notion of a positive/negative operator is defined only for self-adjoint operators.

  • Is \(T^* T\) indeed self-adjoint? We need to prove that \(\left(T^* T\right)^* = T^* T\). We have

    \[ \left(T^*T\right)^* = T^* \left(T^*\right)^* = T^* T \]

  • Note that

    \[ \left\langle T^*T\overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle = \left\langle T\overrightarrow {v}, T\overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle = \left\| T\overrightarrow {v}\right\| ^2 \geq 0, \qquad \forall \overrightarrow {v} \in V \]

    That is, \(T^*T\) is a nonnegative operator.

  • If \(T\) is nondegenerate, then it has trivial kernel. If \(\overrightarrow {v} \neq \overrightarrow {0}\), then \(T\overrightarrow {v} \neq \overrightarrow {0}\) and \(\left\langle T^*T\overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle = \left\| T(v)\right\|^2 > 0\), i.e. \(T\) is positive.

    If \(T\) is degenerate, let \(\overrightarrow {v} \neq \overrightarrow {0}\) be a vector from the kernel. Then \(\left\langle T^*T\overrightarrow {v}, \overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle = \left\| T(v)\right\|^2 = 0\), so \(T^*T\) is not positive.

Any nonzero complex number has several square roots (more precisely, 2). However, any nonnegative number in \(\mathbb {C}\) has a unique nonnegative (i.e. lying in \(\mathbb {R}_+\) as a subset of \(\mathbb {C}\)) square root. An analogous property holds for operators as well.

Theorem 14 Every nonnegative operator \(T\) has a unique nonnegative square root, denoted \(\sqrt{T}\). Moreover, if \(T\) is positive, then \(\sqrt{T}\) is also positive.

How can \(\sqrt{T}\) be found? One needs to find an ONB \(\overrightarrow {e}_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}_n\) of eigenvectors of \(T\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots , \lambda_n\). By the properties of nonnegative operators, all numbers \(\lambda_1, \ldots , \lambda_n\) are nonnegative. Then \(\sqrt{T}\) is given by

\[ (\sqrt{T})(\overrightarrow {e}_i) := \sqrt{\lambda _i} \overrightarrow {e}_i \]

Earlier we discussed the analogy between complex numbers and operators. For \(z \in \mathbb {C}\) we have \(z \cdot \overline{z} = \left|z\right|^2\); adjunction of operators is analogous to complex conjugation. Since \(T^*T\) is nonnegative for any \(T\), we can define the modulus of an operator as \(\left|T\right| := \sqrt{T^*T}\). The theorem above guarantees that this definition is well-posed.

Note once again that the modulus of an operator is necessarily a nonnegative operator. Moreover, if \(T\) is nondegenerate, then \(T^*T\) is also nondegenerate, hence positive, hence \(\left|T\right|\) is also positive.

3.6 Polar decomposition

We have already discussed the analogy between complex numbers \(z\) with \(\left|z\right| = 1\) and unitary operators. Continuing this analogy, recall that any complex number \(z\) can be represented as

\[ z = s \cdot r, \]

where \(s\) lies on the unit circle (that is, \(s = e^{i\arg z}\)), and \(r = \left|z\right|\). If \(z \ne 0\), this representation is unique: \(s = z \cdot \left|z\right|^{-1}\) and

\[ z = \left(z \cdot \left|z\right|^{-1}\right) \cdot \left|z\right| \]

This analogy suggests that an arbitrary operator \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) can be represented as the product of a unitary operator and the nonnegative operator \(\left|T\right|\). This is indeed the case: the result is known as the polar decomposition. It gives a beautiful and useful representation of an arbitrary operator as a product of two well-studied objects:

  • a unitary operator,

  • a nonnegative operator.

Each of these belongs to a class of operators whose properties are well known (see the theorems above).

Theorem 15 (Polar decomposition) Let \(T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\). Then there exists a unitary operator \(S \in \mathcal{L}(V)\) and a unique nonnegative operator \(R\) such that

\[ T = S R \]

Here \(R = \left|T\right|\).

If, in addition, \(T\) is nondegenerate, then \(R = \left|T\right|\) is positive and the unitary operator \(S\) indicated above is unique: \(S = T \cdot \left|T\right|^{-1}\).

ExampleExample 22

Prove the polar decomposition for the case when \(T\) is nondegenerate.

SolutionSolution

  • Let us first prove the uniqueness of such a decomposition (if it exists). If \(T= SR\), where \(S\) is unitary and \(R\) is nonnegative (in particular, self-adjoint: \(R^* = R\)), then

    \[ T^* T= R^* \cdot \underbrace{S^* \circ S}_{=I} \circ R = R^* \circ R = R^2, \]

    where \(T^* T\) and \(R\) are nonnegative operators. By theorem Theorem 14, \(R\) is uniquely determined, with \(R = \sqrt{T^*T} = \left|T\right|\).

    Furthermore, \(T\) is nondegenerate, so \(\left|T\right|\) is also nondegenerate, hence \(S\) is uniquely determined: \(S = T \cdot \left|T\right|^{-1}\).

    In the course of proving uniqueness we obtained expressions for \(R\) and for \(S\). Let us check that they indeed satisfy the required conditions. We have already shown that \(R = \left|T\right|\) is positive. Let us now check that \(S\) is unitary.

  • We have

    \[ S^*S = \underbrace{\left(\left|T\right|^{-1}\right)^{*}}_{=\left(\left|T\right|^{*}\right)^{-1}} \cdot T^* \cdot T \cdot \left|T\right|^{-1} = I \cdot I = I \]

    That is, \(S\) does indeed turn out to be unitary.

What does the polar decomposition look like for matrices of operators?

  • Case \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\): any real matrix \(A\) can be uniquely represented as a product \(U \cdot B\), where \(B\) is symmetric positive semidefinite, and \(U\) is an orthogonal matrix.

  • Case \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {C}\): any nondegenerate complex matrix \(A\) can be uniquely represented as a product \(U \cdot B\), where \(B\) is Hermitian positive semidefinite, and \(U\) is a unitary matrix.

4 Bilinear and Quadratic Functions

In this section \(V\) is a finite-dimensional Euclidean space (\(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\)).

Let \(V\) be a Euclidean space, \(b: V \times V \rightarrow \mathbb {R}\) a symmetric bilinear function. A self-adjoint operator \(T_b\) is called the operator associated with \(b\) if

\[ b(\overrightarrow {u}, \overrightarrow {v})= \left\langle \overrightarrow {u}, T_b\overrightarrow {v} \right\rangle \]

for all \(\overrightarrow {u}, \overrightarrow {v} \in V\).

If \(B\) is the matrix of \(b\) in a basis with Gram matrix \(\Gamma\), then the operator \(T_b\) associated with \(b\) has matrix \(A=\Gamma^{-1} B\) in this basis. In particular, in an ONB \(A = B\). If an ONB diagonalizing \(T_b\) is found, it will also diagonalize the bilinear form \(b\).

ExampleExample 26

Matrices of the quadratic functions:

\[ F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad G = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 8 \\ 8 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \]

Both leading principal minors of the first matrix (\(1\) and \(2\)) are positive, hence, by Sylvester’s criterion, the function \(f\) is positive definite. On the other hand, we see that \(\operatorname {det}\left(G\right) = -40\), i.e. \(g\) is not positive definite. \(f\) can be used as an inner product. The Gram matrix of this inner product in the original basis:

\[ \Gamma = F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \]

The problem now reduces to finding an ONB (with respect to the inner product \(f\)) in which \(g\) has diagonal form.

Let \(T_g\) be the self-adjoint operator associated with \(g\). It has matrix

\[ A = \Gamma ^{-1} \cdot G = F^{-1} \cdot G \]

We now need to find an ONB of eigenvectors for \(T_g\). In it we will get exactly \(G' = \Gamma ' \cdot A' = A' = \operatorname {diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)\).

  • We look for the eigenvalues of the operator \(T_g\). We have

    \[ \chi _{T_g}(t) = \operatorname {det}\left(A - tE\right) = \operatorname {det}\left(F^{-1}G - tE\right) \]

    Here one could compute \(F^{-1}\), multiply it by \(G\), then find \(\chi_{T_g}(t)\) and its roots. However, to find the roots of the characteristic polynomial one can avoid computing the inverse matrix \(F^{-1}\):

    \[ \chi _{T_g}(t) = \operatorname {det}\left(F^{-1}G - tE\right) = \operatorname {det}\left(F^{-1}G - tF^{-1}F\right) = \operatorname {det}\left(F^{-1}\right) \cdot \operatorname {det}\left(G - tF\right) \]

    \[ \begin{align} \operatorname {det}\left(G - tF\right) & = \det \begin{pmatrix} 4 -t & 8 - t \\ 8 - t & 6 - 3t \end{pmatrix} = \left(3t^2 -18t + 24\right) - \left(t^2 - 16t + 64\right) = \\ & = 2t^2 -2t - 40 = 2(t^2 - t - 20) = 2(t -5 )(t + 4) \end{align} \]

    \[ A - \lambda E = F^{-1}\left(G - \lambda F\right) \]

    \[ \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 3 \\ 3 & -9 \end{pmatrix} \overrightarrow {u}_1 = \overrightarrow {0} \; \Rightarrow \; \text{FSS: } \overrightarrow {u}_1 = \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

    \[ \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 12 \\ 12 & 18 \end{pmatrix} \overrightarrow {u}_2 = \overrightarrow {0} \; \Rightarrow \; \text{FSS: } \overrightarrow {u}_2 =\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

ExampleExample 28

Give an example of a pair of quadratic functions \(f\), \(g\) that cannot be simultaneously brought to diagonal form.

SolutionSolution

If at least one of the functions is positive or negative definite, then simultaneous diagonalization is possible. We need both functions to be neither positive definite nor negative definite.

Let \(f\), \(g\) have, in the standard basis, matrices

\[ F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad G = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

Suppose that they can be diagonalized in some basis, and that the change-of-basis matrix to this basis has the form

\[ C = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \]

That is, the following matrices must have diagonal form:

\[ C^T F C = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 - c^2 & ab - cd \\ ab - cd & b^2 - d^2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \text{ and } \quad C^TGC = \begin{pmatrix} 2ac & ad + bc \\ ad + bc & 2bd \end{pmatrix}. \]

Diagonality conditions:

\[ \begin{cases} ab - cd = 0 \\ ad + bc = 0 \end{cases} \]

Hence, we have

\[ \begin{cases} d \cdot \left(ad + bc\right) + b \cdot \left(ab - cd \right) = 0 \\ b \cdot \left(ad + bc\right) - d \cdot \left(ab - cd \right) = 0 \\ \end{cases} \iff \begin{cases} a(b^2 + d^2 ) = 0 \\ c (b^2 + d^2 ) = 0 \end{cases} \]

At least one of \(a\), \(c\) must be nonzero for the change-of-basis matrix \(C\) to be nondegenerate. But then it follows that \(b^2 + d^2 = 0\) and \(b = d = 0\). This means \(C\) is degenerate. Contradiction.
