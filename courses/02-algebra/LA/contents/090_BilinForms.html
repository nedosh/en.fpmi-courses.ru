1 Bilinear and quadratic forms in a vector space over an arbitrary field

1.1 Bilinear forms

Definition 1 Let \(V\) be a vector space over a field \(\mathbb {F}\) (typically, \(\mathbb {F}\) is either the real or the complex numbers). A bilinear function (or bilinear form) on \(V\) is a map \(b : V \times V \to \mathbb {F}\) that is linear in each argument. That is,

\[ \begin{align} b(\alpha \overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) & = \alpha \cdot b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}), & b(\overrightarrow {x}_1 + \overrightarrow {x}_2, \overrightarrow {y}) = b(\overrightarrow {x}_1, \overrightarrow {y}) + b(\overrightarrow {x}_2, \overrightarrow {y}) \\ b(\overrightarrow {x}, \beta \overrightarrow {y}) & = \beta b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}), & b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}_1 + \overrightarrow {y}_2) = b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}_1) + b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}_2) \end{align} \]

for any \(\alpha , \beta \in \mathbb {F}\), \(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}_1, \overrightarrow {x}_2, \overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {y}_1, \overrightarrow {y}_2 \in V\).

We denote the set of all bilinear forms on \(V\) by \(V^{(2)}\).

If \(V\) is equipped with a basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\), then the value of a bilinear function on an arbitrary pair of vectors \(\overrightarrow {x}\), \(\overrightarrow {y}\) is completely determined by their coordinates in the basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\) and by the values of the bilinear function \(b\) on the basis vectors \(b(\overrightarrow {e}_i, \overrightarrow {e}_j) =: b_{ij}\), \(i,j \in \left\{ 1, \ldots , n\right\}\). Indeed, if \([\overrightarrow {x}]^T = (x_1, \ldots , x_n)^T\), \([\overrightarrow {y}]^T = (y_1, \ldots , y_n)^T\) are the coordinates of \(\overrightarrow {x}\), \(\overrightarrow {y}\) in this basis, then by linearity we have

\[ \begin{align} b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) & = b\left(\sum _{i=1}^n x_i \overrightarrow {e}_i, \; \sum _{j=1}^n y_j \overrightarrow {e}_j\right) = \sum _{i,j=1}^n x_iy_j \cdot b(\overrightarrow {e}_i, \overrightarrow {e}_j) = \\ & = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_n \end{pmatrix} \cdot \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} b(\overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_1) & b(\overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_2) & \cdots & b(\overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_n) \\ b(\overrightarrow {e}_2, \overrightarrow {e}_1) & b(\overrightarrow {e}_2, \overrightarrow {e}_2) & \cdots & b(\overrightarrow {e}_2, \overrightarrow {e}_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ b(\overrightarrow {e}_n, \overrightarrow {e}_1) & b(\overrightarrow {e}_n, \overrightarrow {e}_2) & \cdots & b(\overrightarrow {e}_n, \overrightarrow {e}_n) \end{pmatrix}}_{=: B} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{pmatrix} = \\ & = [\overrightarrow {x}]^T \cdot B \cdot [\overrightarrow {y}] \end{align} \]

The matrix \(B \in \mathbf{F}^{n \times n}\) is called the matrix of the bilinear form \(b\) in the basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\). Note that once a basis is fixed, a bilinear form is expressed as a polynomial in the coordinates of the vectors, and every monomial has degree exactly \(2\).

Theorem 1 Let \(\overrightarrow {e}_1, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}_n\) be a basis of \(V\). Then the map \(V^{(2)} \to \mathbf{F}^{n\times n}\) sending a bilinear form to its matrix is an isomorphism of vector spaces.

As a corollary of the theorem above we get

\[ \operatorname {dim}V^{(2)} = \operatorname {dim}\mathbf{F}^{n\times n} = n^2 = \left(\operatorname {dim}V\right)^2 \]

If \((\overrightarrow {e}'_i)_1^n\) is another basis of \(V\), and \(S\) is the change-of-basis matrix from \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\) to \((\overrightarrow {e}'_i)_1^n\), then the matrix of \(b\) in the new basis can be found as follows:

\[ B' = S^T \cdot B \cdot S \tag{1}\]

Note that, unlike the matrix of an operator, which transforms as \(A' = S^{-1}AS\), the old matrix of a bilinear form is multiplied on the left not by \(S^{-1}\), but by \(S^T\). In particular, the determinant of the matrix \(B\) is no longer an invariant, unlike the determinant of \(A\), since

\[ \left|B'\right| = \left|S^T\right|\left|B\right|\left|S\right| = \left|S\right|^2 \cdot \left|B\right| \]

and \(\left|S\right|\) may be different from \(1\) and \(-1\).

On the other hand, the following are invariants:

  • the rank \(\operatorname {rk}\left(B\right)\),

  • the sign of the determinant \(\operatorname {sgn}(\left|B\right|)\), if the field \(\mathbb {F}\) is the field of real numbers.

1.2 Symmetric bilinear forms

A bilinear function \(b\) is called symmetric if \(b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) = b(\overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x})\) for any \(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \in V\).

ExampleExample 4

Prove that a bilinear form is symmetric if and only if its matrix \(B\) in some (and hence in every) basis is symmetric: \(B^T = B\).

SolutionSolution

If \(b\) is symmetric, then \(b_{i,j} = b(\overrightarrow {e}_i, \overrightarrow {e}_j) = b(\overrightarrow {e}_j, \overrightarrow {e}_i) = b_{j,i}\), i.e., \(B^T = B\) in every basis. Conversely, suppose \(B = B^T\) in some basis. A number can be viewed as a \(1 \times 1\) matrix, which coincides with its own transpose, hence \[ b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) = [\overrightarrow {x}]^T B [\overrightarrow {y}] = \left([\overrightarrow {x}]^T B [\overrightarrow {y}]\right)^T = [\overrightarrow {y}]^T B^T [\overrightarrow {x}] = [\overrightarrow {y}]^T B [\overrightarrow {x}] = b(\overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x}) \]

1.3 Quadratic forms

Definition 2 A quadratic function on \(V\) is a function \(q: V \to \mathbb {F}\) for which there exists a symmetric bilinear function \(b: V \times V \to \mathbb {F}\) such that

\[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}), \quad \forall \overrightarrow {x} \in V \]

A quadratic function is sometimes called a quadratic form.

Mn-remarkRemark

A symmetric bilinear function \(b\) is uniquely recovered from its quadratic function \(q\) by the polarization formula: \[ b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) = \frac{1}{2}\bigg(q(\overrightarrow {x} + \overrightarrow {y}) - q(\overrightarrow {x}) - q(\overrightarrow {y})\bigg) \] (expand \(q(\overrightarrow {x} + \overrightarrow {y}) = b(\overrightarrow {x} + \overrightarrow {y}, \overrightarrow {x} + \overrightarrow {y})\) using bilinearity and use symmetry). In practice this amounts to a simple rule: in the matrix \(B\), the coefficient of \(x_i^2\) goes on the diagonal, while the coefficient of \(x_i x_j\) (\(i \neq j\)) is split evenly between positions \((i,j)\) and \((j,i)\).

ExampleExample 5

Write down the matrix of the bilinear function \[ b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) = 2 x_1 y_1 - x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1 - 5 x_2 y_2 \] in a two-dimensional vector space and write down the corresponding quadratic function.

SolutionSolution

We assume that some basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_2)\) of the 2-dimensional space \(V\) is given, and \(x_1, x_2, y_1,y_2\) are the coordinates of the vectors \(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y} \in V\) in this basis. The matrix of \(b\) in this basis:

\[ B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & -5 \end{pmatrix} \]

The bilinear function in matrix form:

\[ b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & -5 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{pmatrix} \]

The quadratic function \(q\) corresponding to \(b\):

\[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}) = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & -5 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = 2x_1^2 - 2x_1x_2 - 5x_2^2 \]

ExampleExample 6

Recover the symmetric bilinear function in a two-dimensional vector space from the quadratic function \[ q(\overrightarrow {x})=-18 x_1 x_2+9 x_2^2 \] and write down its matrix.

SolutionSolution

By the polarization rule, the coefficient of \(x_1 x_2\) is split evenly between the symmetric positions: \[ b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {y}) = -9 x_1y_2 - 9x_2y_1 + 9x_2y_2, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -9 \\ -9 & 9 \end{pmatrix} \] Check: \(b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}) = -9x_1x_2 - 9x_2x_1 + 9x_2^2 = -18x_1x_2 + 9x_2^2 = q(\overrightarrow {x})\).

ExampleExample 7

The quadratic function \[ q(\overrightarrow {x})=25 x_1^2-14 x_1 x_2+2 x_2^2 \] is given in the basis \(\left\{ \overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_2\right\}\). Write down this quadratic function in the basis \(\left\{ \overrightarrow {e}_1^{\prime }, \overrightarrow {e}_2^{\prime }\right\}\), where \[ \overrightarrow {e}_1^{\prime }=\overrightarrow {e}_1+\overrightarrow {e}_2, \overrightarrow {e}_2^{\prime }=-\overrightarrow {e}_1+\overrightarrow {e}_2 \]

SolutionSolution

Let \(b\) be the bilinear function corresponding to the quadratic function \(q\). Then its matrix in the basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_1, \overrightarrow {e}_2)\):

\[ B = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & -7 \\ -7 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \]

The change-of-basis matrix to the primed basis, and the matrix of the quadratic function \(b\) in the primed basis:

\[ S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B' = S^TBS = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 25 & -7 \\ -7 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 13 & -23 \\ -23 & 41 \end{pmatrix} \]

Let \(\overrightarrow {x} = x_1'\overrightarrow {e}_1' + x_2'\overrightarrow {e}_2'\), i.e., \(x_1', x_2'\) are the coordinates of \(\overrightarrow {x}\) in the new, primed basis. Then

\[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = b(\overrightarrow {x}, \overrightarrow {x}) = \begin{pmatrix} x'_1 & x'_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 13 & -23 \\ -23 & 41 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x'_1 \\ x'_2 \end{pmatrix} = 13(x'_1)^2 - 46x_1'x_2' + 41(x_2')^2 \]

2 Bilinear and quadratic functions in a real vector space

In this chapter \(\mathbb {F} = \mathbb {R}\).

2.1 Positive and negative definite quadratic functions

Definition 3 A quadratic form \(q\) on the space \(V\) is called positive definite if

\[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) > 0, \qquad \forall \overrightarrow {x} \in V, \; \overrightarrow {x} \neq \overrightarrow {0} \]

\(q\) is called positive semidefinite (also called nonnegative definite) if the strict inequality above is replaced by a non-strict one:

\[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) \geq 0, \qquad \forall \overrightarrow {x} \in V, \; \overrightarrow {x} \neq \overrightarrow {0} \]

The notions of negative definiteness and negative semidefiniteness are introduced analogously.

2.2 The law of inertia, and reduction of a form to canonical form

Theorem 2 (Sylvester’s law of inertia) Let \(q: V \to \mathbb {R}\) be a quadratic form. Then there exists a basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\) of \(V\) in which the form takes the diagonal form

\[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = x_1^2 + \cdots + x_{r_+}^2 - x_{r_+ + 1}^2 - \cdots - x_{r_++r_-}^2 = \sum _{i=1}^{r_+} x_i^2 - \sum _{j=r_++1}^{r_++r_-}x_j^2, \tag{2}\]

where \((x_i)_1^n\) are the coordinates of the vector \(\overrightarrow {x}\) in this basis and \(r_+ \leq r_++r_- \leq \operatorname {dim}(V) = n\). Equivalently, one can say that the matrix of the bilinear form \(b\) corresponding to \(q\) takes, in this basis, the form

\[ B = \left(\begin{array}{cccc|cccc|ccc}& 1 & 2 & \cdots & r_+ & r_++1 & r_++2 & \cdots & r_++r_- & r_++r_-+1 & \cdots & n \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & & & & 0 & & \\ 2 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & & & & & & & \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ r_+ & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & \\ \hline r_++1 & 0 & & & & -1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & & \\ r_++2 & & & & & 0 & -1 & \cdots & 0 & & & \\ \vdots & & & & & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ r_++r_- & & & & & 0 & 0 & \cdots & -1 & & & \\ \hline r_++r_- + 1 & 0 & & & & 0 & & & & 0 \\ \vdots & \\ n \\ \end{array}\right)= \left(\begin{array}{cccc|cccc|ccc}I_{r_+} & & & & 0 & & & & 0 \\ \hline 0 & & & & -I_{r_-} & & & & 0 & & \\ \hline 0 & & & & 0 & & & & 0 & & \\ \end{array}\right) \]

Moreover, the numbers \(r_+, r_- \in \mathbb {N}\) do not depend on the choice of the basis \((\overrightarrow {e}_i)_1^n\) diagonalizing the form \(q\) (there may be many such bases). That is, \(r_+\), \(r_-\) are invariants of the quadratic form, on a par with its rank or the sign of its determinant.

The form Equation 2 of a quadratic form is called canonical. The numbers \(r_+\) and \(r_-\) from the theorem are called the positive and negative indices of inertia, respectively, of the quadratic form \(b\). This is where the theorem gets its name.

It turns out that \(r_+\), \(r_-\) are the dimensions of the maximal subspaces \(U\), \(W\) of \(V\) on which the form \(q\) is positive definite and negative definite, respectively. That is,

\[ \begin{align} q(\overrightarrow {y}) & > 0, \quad \forall \overrightarrow {y} \in U, \; \overrightarrow {y} \neq \overrightarrow {0} \\ q(\overrightarrow {w}) & < 0, \quad \forall \overrightarrow {w} \in W, \; \overrightarrow {w} \neq \overrightarrow {0} \end{align} \]

Note that \(r_+ + r_- = \operatorname {rk}\left(B\right)\), i.e., the sum of the positive and negative indices of inertia equals the rank of the quadratic form. The difference \(r_+ - r_-\) is called the signature of the quadratic form \(q\).

ExampleExample 8

Reduce the given quadratic form to canonical form using either Lagrange’s method or elementary transformations of its matrix. Find the rank, the positive and negative indices of inertia, and the signature of this form: \[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = x_1^2 + 4x_1x_3 + x_2^2 + 2x_2x_3 + 4x_3^2 \]

SolutionSolution

There are 2 main methods.

  • Lagrange’s method for reducing a quadratic form to canonical form consists in successively completing the square. Namely, if the form contains the square of some coordinate, then we must complete the square using all the terms involving that coordinate. In our case we have \(x_1^2\). Write out all the terms containing \(x_1\): \(x_1^2 + 4x_1x_3\). Complete the square:

    \[ x_1^2 + 4x_1x_3 = (x_1 + 2x_3)^2 - 4x_3^2 \]

    The form becomes:

    \[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = (x_1 + 2x_3)^2 - 4x_3^2 + x_2^2 + 2x_2x_3 + 4x_3^2 = (x_1 + 2x_3)^2 + x_2^2 + 2x_2x_3 \]

    Consider the part of the form remaining after completing the square with \(x_1\): \(x_2^2 + 2x_2x_3\). We have \(x_2^2\), so let us complete the square with leading term \(x_2\):

    \[ x_2^2 + 2x_2x_3 = (x_2 + x_3)^2 - x_3^2 \]

    We obtain

    \[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = (x_1 + 2x_3)^2 + (x_2 + x_3)^2 - x_3^2 \]

    Introduce new coordinates by the formula:

    \[ x'_1 = x_1 + 2x_3, \qquad x'_2 = x_2 + x_3, \qquad x'_3 = x_3 \]

    The matrix \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) is nonsingular, so this is indeed a change of coordinates. In the new coordinates the form takes the canonical form

    \[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = \left(x'_1\right)^2 + \left(x'_2\right)^2 -\left(x'_3\right)^2 \]

    We have

    \[ r_+ = 2, \quad r_- = 1, \quad \operatorname {rk}\left(q\right) = r_+ + r_- = 3, \quad r_+ - r_- = 1 \]

    Note that

    \[ \begin{pmatrix} x'_1 \\ x'_2 \\ x'_3 \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}_{ =: C} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} \]

    The matrix \(C\) lets us pass from the old coordinates to the new ones. That is, it is the change-of-basis matrix from the new (canonical) basis to the old (original) one (don’t get confused!). To obtain the change-of-basis matrix from the original basis to the canonical one, we need to find the inverse matrix:

    \[ S = C^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}^{-1}= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

    We have

    \[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = B' = S^TBS = S^T \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \cdot S \]

    where \(B'\) is the matrix of the bilinear form in the canonical basis, and \(B\) is the matrix \(B\) in the original basis.

  • The method of elementary transformations of the matrix. Note that to obtain the canonical form, it suffices to find the change-of-basis matrix \(S\) to the new basis. Indeed, \(B' = S^T BS\). Here \(S\) is a nonsingular square matrix, so it can be obtained from the identity matrix by elementary column transformations.

    Every elementary column transformation of a matrix corresponds to multiplying the matrix on the right by some matrix. For example, adding to the first column of an arbitrary matrix \(A\) its third column with coefficient \(-5\) corresponds to multiplying on the right by the matrix

    \[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -5 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

    That is, \(S = S_1 \cdot S_2 \cdot \cdots \cdot S_p\), where \(S_i\) is the matrix of an elementary column transformation. Then

    \[ \begin{align} B' & = S^TBS = S_p^T \cdot \cdots \cdot S_2^T \cdot S_1^T \cdot B \cdot S_1 \cdot S_2 \cdot \cdots \cdot S_p = \\ & = S^T_p \cdot \left(S_{p-1}^T\cdot \cdots \cdot \left(S_2^T \cdot \left(S_1^T \cdot B \cdot S_1\right) \cdot S_2\right) \cdot \cdots \cdot S_{p-1}\right) \cdot S_p \end{align} \]

    If the matrix \(S_i\) corresponded to an elementary column transformation, then \(S_i^T\) corresponds to the analogous elementary row transformation. Finding the diagonal matrix \(B'\) with \(1\), \(-1\) and \(0\) on the diagonal amounts to successively performing symmetric elementary transformations on the rows and columns.

    We have

    \[ \begin{aligned} B & = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \sim \\ & \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \\ & \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

    We have arrived at the canonical form! Here

    \[ S_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{pmatrix}, \quad S_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{pmatrix}, \quad S = S_1S_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

    We obtained the same change-of-basis matrix as in Lagrange’s method.

AnswerAnswer

  • Canonical form: \(q(\overrightarrow {x}) = \left(x'_1\right)^2 + \left(x'_2\right)^2 -\left(x'_3\right)^2\);

  • Positive index of inertia: \(2\);

  • Negative index of inertia: \(1\);

  • Rank: \(3\);

  • Signature: \(1\).

ExampleExample 9

Reduce the given quadratic form to canonical form using either Lagrange’s method or elementary transformations of its matrix. Find the rank, the positive and negative indices of inertia, and the signature of this form: \[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = x_1x_2 + x_2x_3 + x_1x_3 \]

SolutionSolution

  • Lagrange’s method. In this case there are no squares in the form. In such a case the following intermediate substitution can help:

    \[ x_1 = \tilde{x}_1 + \tilde{x}_2, \quad x_2 = \tilde{x}_1 - \tilde{x}_2, \quad x_3 = \tilde{x}_3 \]

    Then

    \[ \begin{align} q(\overrightarrow {x}) & = \left(\tilde{x}_1 + \tilde{x}_2\right)\left(\tilde{x}_1 - \tilde{x}_2\right) + \left(\tilde{x}_1 - \tilde{x}_2\right)\tilde{x}_3 + \left(\tilde{x}_1 + \tilde{x}_2\right)\tilde{x}_3 = \\ & = \tilde{x}_1^2 - \tilde{x}_2^2 + 2\tilde{x}_1\tilde{x}_3 \end{align} \]

    Complete the square with leading term \(\tilde{x}_1\):

    \[ \tilde{x}_1^2 + 2\tilde{x}_1\tilde{x}_3 = \left(\tilde{x}_1 + \tilde{x}_3\right)^2 - \tilde{x}_3^2 \]

    We have

    \[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = \left(\tilde{x}_1 + \tilde{x}_3\right)^2 - \tilde{x}_2^2 - \tilde{x}_3^2 \]

    New substitution:

    \[ \begin{cases} x'_1 = \tilde{x}_1 + \tilde{x}_3 \\ x'_2 = \tilde{x}_2 \\ x'_3 = \tilde{x}_3 \\ \end{cases} \]

    In the new coordinates the quadratic form takes the canonical form:

    \[ q(\overrightarrow {x}) = \left(x'_1\right)^2 - \left(x'_2\right)^2 - \left(x'_3\right)^2 \]

    Indices of inertia, rank, signature:

    \[ r_+ = 1, \quad r_- = 2, \quad \operatorname {rk}\left(q\right) = r_+ + r_- = 3, \quad r_+ - r_- = -1 \]

  • The method of elementary transformations of the matrix. We have

    \[ \hspace{-20em}\begin{aligned} B & = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \\ & \sim \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sqrt{2}/2 & \sqrt{2}/2 \\ \sqrt{2}/2 & 0 & \sqrt{2}/2 \\ \sqrt{2}/2 & \sqrt{2}/2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \\ & \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \\ & \sim \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -1/2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & -1/2 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \\ & \sim \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & -1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \sim \\ & \sim \begin{pmatrix} 2/\sqrt{2} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -\sqrt{2}/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2/\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

AnswerAnswer

  • Canonical form: \(q(\overrightarrow {x}) = \left(x'_1\right)^2 - \left(x'_2\right)^2 -\left(x'_3\right)^2\);

  • Positive index of inertia: \(1\);

  • Negative index of inertia: \(2\);

  • Rank: \(3\);

  • Signature: \(-1\).

3 Sylvester’s criteria for positive/negative \ (semi)definiteness of a quadratic form

Let a square matrix of order \(n\in \mathbb {N}\) be given. A minor of the matrix of order \(k \leq n\) is called a leading principal (corner) minor if it is formed by the first \(k\) rows and the first \(k\) columns of the matrix. Note that a matrix of order \(n\) has exactly \(n\) leading principal minors.

Theorem 3 (Sylvester’s criterion for positive definiteness) A quadratic form is positive definite if and only if the leading principal minors of its matrix (in any basis) are positive.

ExampleExample 10

Find all values of the parameter \(\alpha\) for which the quadratic function \[ q(\overrightarrow {x})=x_1^2+2 x_2^2+3 x_3^2+2 \alpha x_1 x_2-2 x_1 x_3+4 x_2 x_3 \] is positive definite.

SolutionSolution

The matrix of this quadratic form:

\[ B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \alpha & -1 \\ \alpha & 2 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \]

Leading principal square submatrices:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \alpha \\ \alpha & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \alpha & -1 \\ \alpha & 2 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \]

Their determinants are exactly the leading principal minors. We have

\[ \begin{align} \delta _1 & = 1 \\ \delta _2 & = 2 - \alpha ^2 = -(\alpha - \sqrt{2})(\alpha + \sqrt{2}) \\ \delta _3 & = -3\alpha ^2 - 4\alpha = -3 \alpha \left(\alpha + \frac{4}{3}\right) \end{align} \]

By Sylvester’s criterion, \(q\) is positive definite if and only if

\[ \begin{cases} \delta _1 > 0 \\ \delta _2 > 0 \\ \delta _3 > 0 \\ \end{cases} \iff \begin{cases} (\alpha - \sqrt{2})(\alpha + \sqrt{2}) < 0 \\ \alpha \left(\alpha + \frac{4}{3}\right) < 0 \end{cases} \iff \begin{cases} \alpha \in \left(-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}\right) \\ \alpha \in \left(-\frac{4}{3}, 0\right) \end{cases} \iff \alpha \in \left(-\frac{4}{3}, 0\right) \]

AnswerAnswer

\(\alpha \in \left(-\frac{4}{3}, 0\right)\)

ExampleExample 11

Let \(q\) be a quadratic form, and let \(B\) be its matrix in some basis.

  1. Is it true that if \(q\) is nonnegative definite, then all leading principal minors of \(B\) are nonnegative?

  2. Is it true that if all leading principal minors of \(B\) are nonnegative, then \(q\) is nonnegative definite?

SolutionSolution
Enum-item(1)

Yes.

Enum-item(2)

Not always. Counterexample: the form with matrix

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \]

All leading principal minors of this matrix are zero. However, its canonical form contains a \(-1\), i.e., the form is not nonnegative definite (indeed, \(q(\overrightarrow {e}_2) = -1\), which contradicts the definition of nonnegative definiteness).

In fact, nonnegative definiteness is equivalent to all principal minors being nonnegative. A minor of a square matrix is called principal if it is formed by rows and columns with the same indices. Equivalently: a minor is called principal if its diagonal is part of the diagonal of the original matrix. Clearly, every leading principal minor is a principal minor. However, there are far more principal minors than leading principal ones: a square matrix of order \(n\) has \(2^n - 1\) principal minors, but only \(n\) leading principal ones.

ExampleExample (Problem 32.20(1))

One diagonal entry of the matrix of a positive definite quadratic form is increased. Prove that the determinant of the matrix increases.

SolutionSolution

Let \(B\) be the matrix of a positive definite form, and \(B' = B + t \cdot E^{(i,i)}\), \(t > 0\) (\(t\) is added to the diagonal entry \(b_{i,i}\); \(E^{(i,i)}\) is the matrix unit). The determinant is linear in the \(i\)-th row, so

\[ \operatorname {det}\left(B'\right) = \operatorname {det}\left(B\right) + t \cdot M_{i,i}, \]

where \(M_{i,i}\) is the complementary minor of the entry \((i,i)\) (the cofactor, with sign \((-1)^{i+i} = 1\)): expand \(\operatorname {det}\left(B'\right)\) along the \(i\)-th row and group the terms.

It remains to note that \(M_{i,i} > 0\). Indeed, the submatrix obtained by deleting the \(i\)-th row and the \(i\)-th column is the matrix of the restriction of our quadratic form to the coordinate subspace \(\left\langle \overrightarrow {e}_1, \ldots , \widehat{\overrightarrow {e}_i}, \ldots , \overrightarrow {e}_n\right\rangle\) (without the \(i\)-th basis vector). The restriction of a positive definite form is positive definite, and the determinant of the matrix of a positive definite form is positive (for example, by Sylvester’s criterion, or because in the canonical basis it equals \(1\), and the sign of the determinant is an invariant). Hence \(\operatorname {det}\left(B'\right) = \operatorname {det}\left(B\right) + t M_{i,i} > \operatorname {det}\left(B\right)\).
